# EP-289

## Problem Statement
Is it true that, for all sufficiently large $k$, there exist finite intervals $I_1,\ldots,I_k\subset \mathbb{N}$, distinct, not overlapping or adjacent, with $\lvert I_i\rvert \geq 2$ for $1\leq i\leq k$ such that\[1=\sum_{i=1}^k \sum_{n\in I_i}\frac{1}{n}?\]

## Site Remarks
Erd\H{o}s and Graham posed this in \cite{ErGr80} without the stipulation the intervals be distinct, non-overlapping, or adjacent, but Kovac in the comments has provided a simple argument showing that it is easily possible without this restriction, and likely \cite{ErGr80} just forgot to mention this natural restriction.

As an example representing $2$ rather than $1$, Hickerson and Montgomery, in the solution to AMS Monthly problem E2689 proposed by Hahn, found\[2=\sum_{i=1}^5 \sum_{n\in I_i}\frac{1}{n}\]where $I_1=[2,7]$, $I_2=[9,10]$, $I_3=[17,18]$, $I_4=[34,35]$, and $I_5=[84,85]$.

- 00:26 on 22 Sep 2025 - Vjeko_Kovac:
  In the current formulation of this problem, the intervals are allowed to overlap and even repeat. This formulation is the one literally taken from [EG80] and the same one was formalized in Lean and emphasized in Dogmachine's comment. Let me present a very easy affirmative solution to this problem interpretation. However, afterwards I will explain that this is probably not what Erdős and Graham wanted to ask.
  
  From the following auxiliary result you will immediately realize what makes things easy in this interpretation of the problem.
  
  Lemma. If $M$ is a multiset with elements\[ z+1,z+2,z+3,z+4\in\mathbb{N} \]appearing with respective multiplicities $m_1,m_2,m_3,m_4$ that satisfy\[ m_2 \geq m_1, \ m_3 \geq m_4, \ m_1+m_4\geq \max\{m_2,m_3\}, \]then, for every\[ 0\leq u \leq m_1+m_4 -\max\{m_2,m_3\}, \]the multiset $M$ can be split into precisely $m_1+m_4-u$ integer-intervals of length $\geq2$. (The idea is to vary $u$ in applications.)
  
  For the proof write elements WLOG as $1,2,3,4$. We simply take
  $m_1-m_3+m_4-u$ times interval $\{1,2\}$,
  $m_1-m_2+m_4-u$ times interval $\{3,4\}$,
  $m_3-m_4$ times interval $\{1,2,3\}$,
  $m_2-m_1$ times interval $\{2,3,4\}$,
  $u$ times interval $\{1,2,3,4\}$.
  
  Now we turn back to the problem, which became much easier as we only need to find appropriate multisets that decompose $1$ into the corresponding unit fractions. It might still not be obvious how to do that, so I include the details.
  
  Take four different primes $p_1,p_2,p_3,p_4\geq 10^{10}$. Find positive integers $a_1,\ldots,a_4$ and $b_1,\ldots,b_4$ such that
  \begin{align*}
  \frac{2}{10} & = \frac{a_1}{10p_1} + \frac{b_1}{p_1} + \frac{b_1}{p_1} + \frac{b_1}{p_1},\\
  \frac{3}{10} & = \frac{b_2}{p_2} + \frac{a_2}{10p_2} + \frac{b_2}{p_2} + \frac{b_2}{p_2},\\
  \frac{3}{10} & = \frac{b_3}{p_3} + \frac{b_3}{p_3} + \frac{a_3}{10p_3} + \frac{b_3}{p_3},\\
  \frac{2}{10} & = \frac{b_4}{p_4} + \frac{b_4}{p_4} + \frac{b_4}{p_4} + \frac{a_4}{10p_4}
  \end{align*}
  and
  \begin{align*}
  & \Big|\frac{a_1}{10p_1}-\frac{2}{40}\Big| \leq 0.001, \ \Big|\frac{b_1}{p_1}-\frac{2}{40}\Big| \leq 0.001,\\
  & \Big|\frac{a_2}{10p_2}-\frac{3}{40}\Big| \leq 0.001, \ \Big|\frac{b_2}{p_2}-\frac{3}{40}\Big| \leq 0.001,\\
  & \Big|\frac{a_3}{10p_3}-\frac{3}{40}\Big| \leq 0.001, \ \Big|\frac{b_3}{p_3}-\frac{3}{40}\Big| \leq 0.001,\\
  & \Big|\frac{a_4}{10p_4}-\frac{2}{40}\Big| \leq 0.001, \ \Big|\frac{b_4}{p_4}-\frac{2}{40}\Big| \leq 0.001.
  \end{align*}
  (We can say that the fractions on the left are split into $4$ almost equal parts.) The correct decomposition into multisets is obtained by taking the columns of the above splitting.
  
  Namely, denote\[ N=10p_1p_2p_3p_4. \]We will only describe the first multiset in detail. By the Chinese Remainder Theorem there exists $w\in\mathbb{N}$, $w\in[0,N)$ such that\[ 10p_1|w+1,\ p_2|w+2,\ p_3|w+3,\ p_4|w+4. \]For every $j\in\mathbb{N}$ we can then write
  \begin{align*}
  \frac{a_1}{10p_1} & = \frac{m_1(j)}{jN+w+1},\\
  \frac{b_2}{p_2} & = \frac{m_2(j)}{jN+w+2},\\
  \frac{b_3}{p_3} & = \frac{m_3(j)}{jN+w+3},\\
  \frac{b_4}{p_4} & = \frac{m_4(j)}{jN+w+4},
  \end{align*}
  where the numerators are positive integers.
  Proceeding similarly with the other three columns we obtain
  \begin{align*}
  1 = & \sum_{l=1}^{4} \frac{m_l(j)}{jN+w+l}
  + \sum_{l=1}^{4} \frac{m'_l(j)}{jN+w'+l}\\
  & + \sum_{l=1}^{4} \frac{m''_l(j)}{jN+w''+l}
  + \sum_{l=1}^{4} \frac{m'''_l(j)}{jN+w'''+l},
  \end{align*}
  which is the splitting according to four multisets on consecutive $4$-tuples of integers. The first multiset is on $\{jN+w+l:1\leq l\leq 4\}$ and its multiplicities $m_l(j)$ satisfy conditions of the Lemma for sufficiently large $j$, because:
  $m_2(j)-m_1(j)$ differs from $jN/40$ by at most $0.002jN + O(1)$,
  $m_3(j)-m_4(j)$ differs from $jN/40$ by at most $0.002jN + O(1)$,
  $m_1(j)+m_4(j)-m_2(j)$ differs from $jN/40$ by at most $0.003jN + O(1)$,
  $m_1(j)+m_4(j)-m_3(j)$ differs from $jN/40$ by at most $0.003jN + O(1)$.
  The Lemma then guarantees its decomposition into intervals of length $\geq2$, where the total number of intervals is\[ (p_2p_3p_4a_1 + 10p_1p_2p_3b_4) j + \frac{w+1}{10p_1}a_1 + \frac{w+4}{p_4}b_4 - u, \]where $u$ is allowed to range over $[0,1000N)$ provided that $j$ is large enough.
  Proceeding like that with the other three multisets, we finally get\[ 1 = \sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{n\in I_i}\frac{1}{n}, \]where the total number of intervals $k$ can be any number of the form\[ Cj + D - u \]for some concrete $C,D\in\mathbb{N}$, $C\leq 1000N$, sufficiently large $j\in\mathbb{N}$, and $u\in[0,1000N)$ arbitrary. These cover all sufficiently large positive integers and the proof is complete.
  
  Now, here is my doubt. In [EG80] on p.34, the problem statement is followed by an analogous representation of the number $2$ found by P. L. Montgomery (and independently also by D. Hickerson) in the solution to Monthly problem E2689 proposed by L.-S. Hahn:
  \begin{align*}
  2 = & \underbrace{\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{5} + \frac{1}{6} + \frac{1}{7}}_{I_1}
  + \underbrace{\frac{1}{9} + \frac{1}{10}}_{I_2} \\
  & + \underbrace{\frac{1}{17} + \frac{1}{18}}_{I_3}+ \underbrace{\frac{1}{34} + \frac{1}{35}}_{I_4}+ \underbrace{\frac{1}{84} + \frac{1}{85}}_{I_5}.
  \end{align*}
  It could be that Erdős and Graham were not able to find a single such representation of the number $1$, so they used the example with $2$ instead. Moreover, when one goes to look into the reference, one finds that the said problem was to represent just any integer as $\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{n\in I_i}1/n$ for non-overlapping $I_i$ of length $\geq2$ (restated to match the notation here). In any case, this example hints that Erdős and Graham might have wanted the intervals $I_i$ to be non-overlapping. Moreover, maybe even adjacent intervals (like $\{4,5\}$ and $\{6,7\}$) are not allowed, since their total count ($=k$) is important.
  
  I would be in favor of changing the problem formulation to a nontrivial one (perhaps the most restrictive one), even though I am clueless on how to approach the non-overlapping case. There is plentitude of unit fractions summing to $1$, but not even nearly enough for some pigeonholing to group them into intervals. Some other variants of the above simple proof are in fact possible, for instance to also avoid adjacent intervals, but the same idea is not of much help if we want the intervals to be non-overlapping, or even just mutually different.

## Local Computation
I ran exact randomized searches with integerized harmonic sums (common denominator
lcm(1..Nmax)) for decompositions of 1 into interval blocks.

No exact decomposition was found in these searches for
- Nmax=90, k<=10
- Nmax=140, k<=8
within the tested attempt budgets.

## Local Proof Attempts
Hard point:
This is negative finite evidence only; non-findings under randomized search do
not imply nonexistence for larger ranges or different constructions.

Target:
Determine whether for all sufficiently large \(k\) one can represent \(1\) as a
sum of reciprocal interval-block sums with \(k\) disjoint non-adjacent intervals
of length at least \(2\).
Step A: Interval block algebra
Parameterize interval sums as
\[
H[a,b]=\sum_{n=a}^b\frac1n=\frac{U_{a,b}}{L_{a,b}}
\]
and track denominators under disjoint unions.
Step B: Sparse gluing mechanism
Develop a controlled concatenation that inserts new intervals far away while
preserving exact equality to \(1\) via compensating local modifications.
Step C: Arbitrary-\(k\) inflation
Iterate Step B to increase number of intervals without breaking non-overlap and
non-adjacency.
Concrete Blocking Lemma (Most Critical):
Need a "gap-preserving refinement lemma": from one exact representation with
\(k\) intervals, produce one with \(k+1\) intervals by splitting a block into two
non-adjacent interval blocks plus a finite correction identity.

Lemma 1 (Common-denominator representation):
For finite interval family \(\mathcal I=\{[a_i,b_i]\}\),
\[
\sum_{I\in\mathcal I} H(I)=\frac{A}{L},
\]
where \(L=\mathrm{lcm}\{a_i,\dots,b_i\:\ I\in\mathcal I\}\) and \(A\in\mathbb Z\).
\[
\textit{Proof.}
\]
Each \(H(I)\) is a rational with denominator dividing \(L\); summing yields
integer numerator over \(L\).
\(\square\)
Lemma 2 (Disjointness stability under dilation/translation):
If intervals \(I_i\) are pairwise disjoint and non-adjacent, then for any
integer \(M\ge 2\), intervals \(MI_i+t\) remain pairwise disjoint and non-adjacent.
\[
\textit{Proof.}
\]
Scaling by \(M\ge2\) multiplies endpoint gaps by at least \(2\); adding a common
translation preserves differences.
\(\square\)
Lemma 3 (Reduction to one-step inflation):
If every valid \(k\)-interval representation can be transformed into a valid
\(k+1\)-interval representation, then existence for one large \(k_0\) implies
existence for all \(k\ge k_0\).
\[
\textit{Proof.}
\]
Iterate the one-step transformation \(k-k_0\) times.
\(\square\)
