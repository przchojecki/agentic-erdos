# EP-940

## Problem Statement
Let $r\geq 3$. A number $n$ is $r$-powerful if for every prime $p$ which divides $n$ we have $p^r\mid n$.

Are there infinitely many integers which are not the sum of at most $r$ many $r$-powerful numbers? Does the set of integers which are the sum of at most $r$ $r$-powerful numbers have density $0$?

## Site Remarks
Erd\H{o}s \cite{Er76d} claims that the claim that the set has density $0$ is 'easy' for $r=2$ (a potential 'easy argument' is given in the comments by Tao; this was first proved in the literature by Baker and Br\"{u}dern \cite{BaBr94}). For $r=3$ it is not even known if those integers which are the sum of at most three cubes has density $0$.

In the Oberwolfach problem book this is recorded in 1986 as a problem of Erd\H{o}s and Ivi\'{c}.

In \cite{Er76d} Erd\H{o}s claims that 'a simple counting argument' implies that there are infinitely many integers which are not the sum of at most $r$ many $r$-powerful numbers, but Schinzel pointed out he made a mistake.

Heath-Brown \cite{He88} has proved that all large numbers are the sum of at most three $2$-powerful numbers, see [941].

See also [1081] for a more refined question concerning the case $r=2$, and [1107] for the case of $r+1$ summands.

- 14:19 on 24 Oct 2025 - TerenceTao (reply depth 1):
  OK, here is an "easy" proof. A powerful number is a product of a square and a cube, so one is asking to count $n \leq x$ of the form $n = c^3 a^2 + d^3 b^2$ for some $a,b,c,d$. This forces $a = O(x^{1/2}/c^{3/2})$ and $b = O(x^{1/2}/d^{3/2})$, so for any fixed $c,d$ the number of such $n$ is $O(x / c^{3/2} d^{3/2})$. This converges in $c,d$, so to get zero density one can restrict attention to the bounded case $c,d=O(1)$ (dominated convergence). But then one is essentially in the sum of two squares case (in a suitable number field).

## Local Computation
- Method: exact SPF classification of \(r\)-powerful numbers up to maxN, then dynamic programming for representability by exactly/at-most \(t\le r\) summands.
- Run: maxN = 5,000,000, \(r\in\{3,4,5\}\).
- Counts of available \(r\)-powerful summands:
 - \(r=3\): 553
 - \(r=4\): 191
 - \(r=5\): 98
- Coverage by at most \(r\) summands (up to 5e6):
 - \(r=3\): 4,371,713 values (0.8743426 density)
 - \(r=4\): 4,618,651 values (0.9237302 density)
 - \(r=5\): 4,683,905 values (0.936781 density)

Finite coverage is very high but not complete at this scale. This supports the additive-basis direction as plausible while leaving global exact statements open.
