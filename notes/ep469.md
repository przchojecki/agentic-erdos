# EP-469

## Problem Statement
Let $A$ be the set of all $n$ such that $n=d_1+\cdots+d_k$ with $d_i$ distinct proper divisors of $n$, but this is not true for any $m\mid n$ with $m<n$. Does\[\sum_{n\in A}\frac{1}{n}\]converge?

## Site Remarks
The integers in $A$ are also known as primitive pseudoperfect numbers and are listed as A006036 (https://oeis.org/A006036) in the OEIS.

The same question can be asked for those $n$ which do not have distinct sums of sets of divisors, but any proper divisor of $n$ does (which are listed as A119425 (https://oeis.org/A119425) in the OEIS).

Benkoski and Erd\H{o}s \cite{BeEr74} ask about these two sets, and also about the set of $n$ that have a divisor expressible as a distinct sum of other divisors of $n$, but where no proper divisor of $n$ has this property.

## Site References

- 20:23 on 26 Aug 2025 - DesmondWeisenberg:
  Let $S \subseteq \mathbb{N}$ be the set of numbers that are NOT of the form $np^k(p + a_1)^{r_1}(p + a_2)^{r_2}\cdots (p + a_t)^{r_t}$, where $0 < a_1 < \cdots < a_t$, where $k \geq 1$ and each $r_i \geq 0$, where $p$ and each $p + a_i$ are prime, and where
  \begin{equation*}
  p > \left(\sum_{0 \leq s_i \leq r_i} a_1^{s_1}\cdots a_t^{s_t}\right)\sigma(n).\end{equation*}
  Then $A \subseteq S$. To prove this, it suffices to prove that if $m$ is a pseudoperfect number of the above form, then $m$ has a proper factor that is also pseudoperfect. If $m$ is pseudoperfect of the above form, then there exist distinct proper factors $d_i$ of $m$ such that
  \begin{equation*}
  d_1 + \cdots + d_l = m.
  \end{equation*}
  Since $m$ is a multiple of $p$, the sum $d_1 + \cdots + d_l$ must be a multiple of $p$ as well. For every possible $(p+1)$-rough component (denoted by exponents $s_1, ..., s_t$), consider the sum of all $d_i$ with that component. If at least one of these $d_i$ is not a multiple of $p$, then the sum $\pmod{p}$ is at least 1 and at most $a_1^{s_1}\cdots a_t^{s_t}\sigma(n)$. So if any $d_i$ is not a multiple of $p$, then we have
  \begin{equation*}
  p \leq \left(\sum_{0 \leq s_i \leq r_i} a_1^{s_1}\cdots a_t^{s_t}\right)\sigma(n),\end{equation*}
  a contradiction. So every $d_i$ is a multiple of $p$, meaning that $\frac{m}{p}$ is also pseudoperfect.
  
  This proves that $A \subseteq S$. I don't know how helpful this is (in particular, I don't know anything about the asymptotic behavior of $S$, though I haven't really looked into it too closely), but I'm leaving this note here just in case it may be useful.

## Local Proof Attempts
Target:
Decide convergence of
\[
\sum_{n\in \mathcal A}\frac1n
\]
where \(\mathcal A\) is primitive pseudoperfect numbers.
A. Concrete Blocking Lemma (Most Critical):
Prove counting upper bound
\[
\#\{n\le x:\ n\in\mathcal A\}\le x^{1-\delta}
\]
for some \(\delta>0\), or stronger logarithmic sparsity.
B. Reduction Lemmas (with proofs):
Lemma B1 (partial summation criterion):
If \(A(x)=\#(\mathcal A\cap[1,x])\), then
\[
\sum_{\substack{n\in\mathcal A\\ n\le X}}\frac1n
=\frac{A(X)}{X}+\int_1^X \frac{A(t)}{t^2}\,dt.
\]
Proof.
Apply Abel summation to indicator sequence of \(\mathcal A\). \(\square\)
Lemma B2 (convergence test):
If \(A(x)\ll x^{1-\delta}\), then \(\sum_{n\in\mathcal A}1/n\) converges.
Proof.
Insert bound into B1: integral \(\int t^{-1-\delta}dt\) converges. \(\square\)
Lemma B3 (A implies convergence):
Lemma A directly implies B2, hence convergence.
Proof.
Immediate substitution. \(\square\)
C. Main unresolved point:
The bottleneck is proving strong sparsity for primitive pseudoperfect numbers, which combine subset-sum and divisor-structure constraints.
