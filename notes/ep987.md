# EP-987

## Problem Statement
Let $x_1,x_2,\ldots \in (0,1)$ be an infinite sequence and let\[A_k=\limsup_{n\to \infty}\left\lvert \sum_{j\leq n} e(kx_j)\right\rvert,\]where $e(x)=e^{2\pi ix}$.

Is it true that\[\limsup_{k\to \infty} A_k=\infty?\]Is it possible for $A_k=o(k)$?

## Site Remarks
This is Problem 7.21 in \cite{Ha74}, where it is attributed to Erd\H{o}s.

Erd\H{o}s \cite{Er64b} remarks it is 'easy to see' that\[\limsup_{k\to \infty}\left(\sup_n\left\lvert \sum_{j\leq n} e(kx_j)\right\rvert\right)=\infty.\]Erd\H{o}s \cite{Er65b} later found a 'very easy' proof that $A_k\gg \log k$ for infinitely many $k$. Clunie \cite{Cl67} proved that $A_k\gg k^{1/2}$ infinitely often, and that there exist sequences with $A_k\leq k$ for all $k$. Tao has independently found a proof that $A_k\gg k^{1/2}$ infinitely often (see the comment section).

Liu \cite{Li69} showed that, for any $\epsilon>0$, $A_k\gg k^{1-\epsilon}$ infinitely often, under the additional assumption that there are only a finite number of distinct points. Clunie observed in the Mathscinet review of \cite{Li69}, however, that under this assumption in fact $A_k=\infty$ infinitely often.

An internal OpenAI model (see \cite{APSSV26b}) has proved that there exists a sequence $(x_n)$ such that\[A_k \ll (k\log k)^{1/2}\]for all $k\geq 1$ (in fact one may replace the $\limsup$ in $A_k$ with a $\sup$).

- 17:27 on 09 Apr 2026 - TerenceTao:
  Here is my attempt at a high level summary of the construction in [APSSV26b], which creates a sequence $x_n$ of points which are very uniformly distributed in the sense that
  $$ |\sum_{j \leq n} e(k x_j)| \ll \sqrt{k \log k}$$
  for all $k, n > 0$. The key difficulty here is the need to get bounds that are uniform for all $n$ with no exceptions; various random or deterministic constructions can easily get this type of bound for most $n$, but these usually generate some gaussian tail outlier events which will typically violate this sort of bound for infinitely many $n$ (basically thanks to the infinite monkey theorem). One can try to get around this by making the $x_j$ exhibit some sort of periodicity, but if the sequence is too periodic then this also causes at least one exponential sum to grow linearly in $n$, which also breaks the construction. So one now needs to look for sequences that are very nearly periodic without actually being periodic.
  
  In my comment back in August I suggested trying to design the sequence so that exponential sums vanish on dyadic intervals, in the sense that
  $$\sum_{2^i m < j \leq 2^i(m+1)} e(k x_j) = 0 \tag{1}$$
  as long as $k$ is not too large compared to the length of the interval $2^i$. The [APSSV26b] construction actually achieves this, and goes further by proving a bound of the shape
  $$\sum_{2^i m < j \leq 2^i(m+1)} e(k x_j) \ll \sqrt{k \log k} \tag{2}$$
  for all $i, m, k$, with a geometric improvement in the error term if the length $2^i$ of the interval is much shorter or much larger than $k$. This is the key bound (Proposition 3.5); the sum $\sum_{j \leq n} e(k x_j)$ can then be controlled by using the binary representation of $n$ to break up $\{1,\dots,n\}$ greedily into dyadic intervals. The critical case occurs when $2^i \asymp k$; the interval is not quite long enough to be able to expect the perfect cancellation (1), but the error bound claimed is of square root cancellation type.
  
  Again, the main issue here is that the bound has to hold uniformly for all $m$, with no exceptions. So a random construction that has a small failure rate for a given choice of $i$ and $k$ can still lead to infinitely many counterexamples if the sums in (2) behave independently in $m$. As discussed above, we thus need a sequence $x_j$ that is random enough to get square root cancellation quite often, yet also close enough to periodic that one does not actually have to verify (2) for all $m$, but merely for some large but finite number of $m$ so that the total failure probability does not get out of control.
  
  As mentioned in the paper, the van der Corput sequence (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Van_der_Corput_sequence), in which $x_n$ is the reversal of the binary representation of $n$ expressed as binary strings past the decimal point, is a good candidate; this sequence is almost $2^J$-periodic for any $J$ in the sense that shifting $n$ by a multiple of $2^J$ only affects $x_n$ by $O(2^{-J})$. If the $x_n$ had this sort of periodicity property, a bound such as (2) only needs to be verified for $m$ of size that is polynomial in $2^i$ and $k$, as the bound for larger $m$ would then be automatic.
  
  When $k$ is small compared to the interval size $2^i$, then the van der Corput sequence $x_j$ is already quite well equidistributed on a dyadic interval $(2^i m, 2^i(m+1)]$ (it basically traverses a coset of the $2^i$th roots of unity), which gives (1) which of course implies (2); the difficulty is when $k$ is comparable to or larger than $2^i$, in which perfect vanishing (1) is no longer expected. The van der Corput sequence is deterministic, so one cannot use randomness to create the required square root cancellation.
  
  What the argument in [APSSV26b] does is randomize the van der Corput sequence with a high-entropy source of random bits, by performing dependent random bit flips to the binary representation, that introduce about $2^i$ bits of entropy in any dyadic block $x_j, j \in (2^i m, 2^i(m+1)]$. This randomization preserves the approximate periodicity and equidistribution properties of the deterministic van der Corput sequence, but introduces enough randomness that one can now get the desired square root cancellation in all remaining cases by standard concentration of measure and union bound arguments.

- 16:49 on 30 Aug 2025 - TerenceTao:
  The answer is yes. Proof: assume not, then there exists $k_0, C$, and $x_1,x_2,\dots$ such that
  $$ \limsup_{n \to \infty} |\sum_{j \leq n} e(kx_j)| \leq C$$
  for all $k \geq k_0$. In particular, for any $K \geq k_0$, there exists $N_K$ such that
  $$ |\sum_{j \leq n} e(kx_j)| \leq C+1$$
  whenever $k_0 \leq k \leq K$ and $n \geq N_K$. In particular, by the triangle inequality we have
  $$ |\sum_{N_K < j \leq N_K + n} e(kx_j)| \leq 2(C+1)$$
  whenever $k_0 \leq k \leq K$ and $n \geq 0$, which we can relabel as
  $$ |\sum_{j \leq n} e(kx_{N_K+j})| \leq 2(C+1). \quad (1)$$
  The key point here is that $n$ is no longer required to be sufficiently large depending on $K$.
  
  By weak compactness (Arzela-Ascoli), we can pass to a subsequence of $K$ where the $x_{N_K+j}$ converge pointwise mod 1 to some limit $y_j \in [0,1)$. We now have
  $$ |\sum_{j \leq n} e(ky_j)| \leq 2(C+1)$$
  for all $k \geq k_0$ and $n \geq 0$. Squaring and averaging in $K$, we see that
  $$ \limsup_{K \to \infty} \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^K |\sum_{j \leq n} e(ky_j)|^2 \leq 4(C+1)^2$$
  for any $n \geq 0$. Expanding out the square, the left-hand side can be computed explicitly to be
  $$ | \{ 1 \leq i,j \leq n: y_i = y_j \} |.$$
  But this is clearly at least $n$; taking $n$ larger than $4(C+1)^2$, we obtain the desired contradiction.

- 18:28 on 30 Aug 2025 - TerenceTao (reply depth 1):
  An alternate, more quantitative, proof avoiding the weak compactness step. For fixed $n,K$, argue as before to get to (1). Now use the identity
  $$ \sum_{k,k' \leq K} |\sum_{j \leq n} e((k-k') x_{N_K+j})|^2 = \sum_{j,j' \leq n} |\sum_{k \leq K} e(k (x_{N_k+j}-x_{N_k+j'}))|^2.$$
  By considering the diagonal $j=j'$, we see that the RHS is at least $nK^2$. On the other hand, from (1) (for $|k-k'| \geq k_0$) and the trivial triangle inequality bound (for $|k-k'| < k_0$) the left-hand side is at most $4(C+1)^2 K^2 + n^2 K (2k_0-1)$. So we have
  $$ nK^2 \leq 4(C+1)^2 K^2 + n^2 K (2k_0-1)$$
  for all $n,K$. Taking $n > 4(C+1)^2$ and then $K$ sufficiently large depending on $C,n,k_0$ we obtain a contradiction.

## Local Proof Attempts
Rigorous identities and bounds:
For fixed \(N\), define
\[
S_N(k):=\sum_{j=1}^N e(kx_j),\qquad e(t):=e^{2\pi i t}.
\]
Lemma 1 (trivial absolute bound):
\[
|S_N(k)|\le N\quad\text{for all }k.
\]
Proof: triangle inequality and \(|e(kx_j)|=1\).
Lemma 2 (pair-correlation expansion):
\[
|S_N(k)|^2=\sum_{a,b\le N} e\!\big(k(x_a-x_b)\big).
\]
Hence for \(K\ge1\),
\[
\frac1K\sum_{k=1}^K |S_N(k)|^2
=N+\sum_{a\ne b}\frac1K\sum_{k=1}^K e\!\big(k(x_a-x_b)\big).
\]
This exact identity separates diagonal mass (\(=N\)) from off-diagonal cancellation.
Consequence for strategy:
To force large limsup behavior, one seeks sequence structures where off-diagonal cancellation is weak for infinitely many \(k\); to force small behavior, one needs strong cancellation uniformly in \(k\).
So EP-987 can be viewed as a quantitative cancellation problem in exponential sums indexed by integer frequencies.
