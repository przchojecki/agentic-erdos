# EP-293

## Problem Statement
Let $k\geq 1$ and let $v(k)$ be the minimal integer which does not appear as some $n_i$ in a solution to\[1=\frac{1}{n_1}+\cdots+\frac{1}{n_k}\]with $1\leq n_1<\cdots <n_k$. Estimate the growth of $v(k)$.

## Site Remarks
Results of Bleicher and Erd\H{o}s \cite{BlEr75} imply $v(k) \gg k!$. It may be that $v(k)$ grows doubly exponentially in $\sqrt{k}$ or even $k$.

An elementary inductive argument shows that $n_k\leq ku_k$ where $u_1=1$ and $u_{i+1}=u_i(u_i+1)$, and hence\[v(k) \leq kc_0^{2^k},\]where\[c_0=\lim_n u_n^{1/2^n}=1.26408\cdots\]is the 'Vardi constant' (small improvements on this are possible as in [148]).

van Doorn and Tang \cite{vDTa25b} have proved that\[v(k)\geq e^{ck^2}\]for some constant $c>0$, and noted a close connection to [304]. In particular, if $N(b)\ll \log\log b$ as in [304] then it is likely the methods of \cite{vDTa25b} prove $v(k) \geq e^{e^{ck}}$ for some $c>0$.

- 16:53 on 08 Dec 2025 - Quanyu Tang:
  A small remark on the upper bound for $v(k)$. The usual estimate $v(k) ≤ k c_0^{2^k}$ can be slightly sharpened to \( v(k) \le c_0^{2^k} +\frac{1}{2}\), where $c_0 = 1.26408\ldots$ is Vardi’s constant. Let \((s_j)_{j\ge1}\) be Sylvester's sequence defined by \(s_1=2,\ s_{j+1}=s_j(s_j-1)+1\). Now take any representation \( 1 = \frac{1}{n_1} + \dots + \frac{1}{n_k}\), \(1 \le n_1 < \dots < n_k\), and set \(q := \sum_{i=1}^{k-1} 1/n_i\). It is well known that Sylvester's sequence gives the best $n$-term Egyptian underapproximation of $1$ for every positive integer $n$ (see, for example, [Soundararajan (https://arxiv.org/abs/math/0502247)]; see also [Nathanson (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022314X22001615)] and [Chu (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0019357724000065)] for further generalisations), hence \(q=\sum_{i=1}^{k-1} \frac{1}{n_i} \le \sum_{j=1}^{k-1} \frac{1}{s_j} = 1 - \frac{1}{s_{k}-1}\). Thus \( \frac{1}{n_k} = 1 - q \ge \frac{1}{s_{k}-1}\), so \(n_k \le s_{k}-1\). In particular every denominator occurring in a $k$-term representation of $1$ is at most \(s_{k}-1\), and therefore\( v(k) \le (s_{k}-1) + 1=s_{k}\). Since \(s_k = \lfloor c_0^{2^k} +\frac{1}{2} \rfloor\), this yields \(v(k) \le c_0^{2^k} +\frac{1}{2}\).
