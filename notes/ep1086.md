# EP-1086

## Problem Statement
Let $g(n)$ be minimal such that any set of $n$ points in $\mathbb{R}^2$ contains the vertices of at most $g(n)$ many triangles with the same area. Estimate $g(n)$.

## Site Remarks
Equivalently, how many triangles of area $1$ can a set of $n$ points in $\mathbb{R}^2$ determine? Erd\H{o}s and Purdy attribute this question to Oppenheim. Erd\H{o}s and Purdy \cite{ErPu71} proved\[n^2\log\log n \ll g(n) \ll n^{5/2},\]and believed the lower bound to be closer to the truth. The upper bound has been improved a number of times - by Pach and Sharir \cite{PaSh92}, Dumitrescu, Sharir, and T\'{o}th \cite{DST09}, Apfelbaum and Sharir \cite{ApSh10}, and Apfaulbaum \cite{Ap13}. The best known bound is\[g(n) \ll n^{20/9}\]by Raz and Sharir \cite{RaSh17}.

Erd\H{o}s and Purdy also ask a similar question about the higher-dimensional generalisations - more generally, let $g_d^{r}(n)$ be minimal such that any set of $n$ points in $\mathbb{R}^d$ contains the vertices of at most $g_d^{r}(n)$ many $r$-dimensional simplices with the same volume.

Erd\H{o}s and Purdy \cite{ErPu71} proved $g_3^2(n) \ll n^{8/3}$, and Dumitrescu, Sharir, and T\'{o}th \cite{DST09} improved this to $g_3^2(n) \ll n^{2.4286}$.
Erd\H{o}s and Purdy \cite{ErPu71} proved $g_6^2(n)\gg n^3$. Purdy \cite{Pu74} proved\[g_4^2(n)\leq g^2_5(n) \ll n^{3-c}\]for some constant $c>0$. An observation of Oppenheim (using a construction of Lenz) detailed in \cite{ErPu71} shows that\[g_{2k+2}^k(n)\geq \left(\frac{1}{(k+1)^{k+1}}+o(1)\right)n^{k+1}\]and Erd\H{o}s and Purdy conjecture this is the best possible.

See also [90] and [755].

## Local Computation
- deep randomized search .
- For each fixed $n$, sampled many distinct integer-grid point sets and computed exact area-multiplicity histograms over all nondegenerate triangles.
- Objective: maximize multiplicity of one area value (i.e., many triangles sharing the same area).

Deep run (DEPTH=4) used:
- box size $95$,
- per-$n$ time budget $12$ seconds,
- $n\in\{18,22,26,30,34\}$,
- total runtime $60$ seconds,
- total candidates evaluated: $813{,}352$,
- estimated triangles scanned: $1{,}541{,}210{,}476$.
Best multiplicities found in this run:
- $n=18$: 18,
- $n=22$: 16,
- $n=26$: 19,
- $n=30$: 25,
- $n=34$: 28.
Normalized values in this finite range are noisy and non-monotone, so they do not distinguish the asymptotic exponent; they provide only finite lower-bound evidence from explicit constructions.

## Local Proof Attempts
Target:
Determine growth of \(g(n)\), the maximum multiplicity of one triangle area in an \(n\)-point planar set.
A. Concrete Blocking Lemma (Most Critical):
Prove a structural theorem: if one area occurs \(M\) times, then the point set has an incidence-rich bipartite line-point decomposition forcing
\[
M \ll n^{2+\varepsilon}
\]
for all \(\varepsilon>0\) (or stronger).
Combined with known constructions, this would narrow the exponent gap sharply.
B. Reduction Lemmas (with proofs):
Lemma B1 (area-line incidence encoding):
Fix ordered pair \((a,b)\). Points \(c\) with \([a,b,c]=A\) lie on at most two lines parallel to \(ab\).
Proof.
Area condition \(|[a,b,c]|=A\) is equivalent to fixed perpendicular distance from \(c\) to line \(ab\), giving two parallel offsets (or one if \(A=0\)). \(\square\)
Lemma B2 (triple counting reduction):
Total number of triangles of area \(A\) can be bounded by summing point-line incidences over these offset lines generated by ordered pairs.
Proof.
For each ordered base \((a,b)\), count admissible \(c\) on the two offset lines from Lemma B1; summing over bases counts all ordered triangles of area \(A\). \(\square\)
Lemma B3 (incidence bound transfer):
Any incidence estimate \(I(P,\mathcal L)\ll n^\alpha\) for the generated line family implies \(g(n)\ll n^\alpha\) up to constants.
Proof.
Apply Lemma B2 and substitute the incidence upper bound. \(\square\)
C. Main unresolved point:
The key gap is controlling multiplicities in the generated line family (many repeated or highly structured lines), where standard Szemeredi-Trotter bounds lose sharpness.
