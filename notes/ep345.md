# EP-345

## Problem Statement
Let $A\subseteq \mathbb{N}$ be a complete sequence, and define the threshold of completeness $T(A)$ to be the least integer $m$ such that all $n\geq m$ are in\[P(A) = \left\{\sum_{n\in B}n : B\subseteq A\textrm{ finite }\right\}\](the existence of $T(A)$ is guaranteed by completeness).

Is it true that there are infinitely many $k$ such that $T(n^k)>T(n^{k+1})$?

## Site Remarks
Erd\H{o}s and Graham \cite{ErGr80} remark that very little is known about $T(A)$ in general. It is known that\[T(n)=1, T(n^2)=128, T(n^3)=12758,\]\[T(n^4)=5134240,\textrm{ and }T(n^5)=67898771.\]Erd\H{o}s and Graham remark that a good candidate for the $n$ in the question are $k=2^t$ for large $t$, perhaps even $t=3$, because of the highly restricted values of $n^{2^t}$ modulo $2^{t+1}$.

- 16:19 on 26 Aug 2025 - StijnC:
  It may well that the answer is negative, i.e., that there aren't infinitely many such $k$ (if there is already even one of them).
  
  \textbf{background}
  The value of $T(n^k)$ is known for $k\le 8$, see [oeis A001661] (https://oeis.org/A001661/list).
  
  In particular, we know that $T(n^8)<T(n^9)$ by the estimate by [Wiener23] (https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/journals/JIS/VOL26/Wiener/wiener3.pdf).
  
  \textbf{Remark on the growth of $T(x^k)$ and the candidates by EG}
  The restrictedness is slightly larger; $n^{2^t} \equiv 0,1 \pmod{2^{t+2}}$ for every $t \ge 2$.
  
  This implies that indeed, when $r \equiv -1 \pmod{2^{t+2}}$, there are combinations with at least $2^{t+2}-1$ odd $2^t$th powers needed.
  From this, one gets immediately a lowerbound for $T(n^{2^t})$ equal to
  $$\sum_{i=1}^{2^{t+2}-1}(2i-1)^{2^t}=(c+o(1)) \int_1^{2^{t+3}} x^{2^{t}} dx=(c+o(1))*2^{(t+3)2^t+3}$$ for some $0.375<c<1/2$.
  
  But one can actually do a better estimate.
  Let $n$ be the solution to $n^k=2^n$, so $$n=\log_2(e) k (\ln(k)+\ln(\ln(k))+o(1))=k (\log_2(k)+\log_2(\log_2(k)+O(1)).$$ Now, since there are only $2^{n-1}$ combinations of $1^k$ up to $(n-1)^k$, there is definitely a number in the range $[2^{n-1},2^n]$ which cannot be represented as the sum of different $k^{th}$ powers.
  
  Thus $T(x^k)>2^{n-1}=2^{ k (\log_2(k)+\log_2(\log_2(k))+O(1))}.$
  When $k=2^t$, we notice that this is of the form $\Theta\left(2^{ 2^t(t+\log_2(t))} \right)$,
  which is larger than $\Theta \left( 2^{(t+3)2^t}\right)$.
  
  The presented lower bound is far off from the correct bound, but also grows by a factor which is at least $ek \log_2(k).$

## Local Proof Attempts
I reviewed the strongest concrete values recorded in the background and compared
their monotonic pattern.

Hard point:
The question is about infinitely many reversals at large k, far beyond the
currently known finite data.

Target:
Decide whether infinitely many \(k\) satisfy
\[
T(n^k)>T(n^{k+1}),
\]
where \(T(\cdot)\) is the completeness threshold.
Step A: Cross-\(k\) transfer inequalities
Relate representability by \(k\)-th powers and \((k+1)\)-th powers through
explicit comparison maps.
Step B: Threshold monotonicity defect analysis
Identify mechanisms causing possible reversals despite apparent early monotonicity.
Step C: Infinite occurrence vs eventual monotonicity
Prove one of: infinitely many reversals, or eventual one-direction monotonicity.
Concrete Blocking Lemma (Most Critical):
Need an asymptotic comparison lemma controlling distortion between \(k\)-power and
\((k+1)\)-power sumsets near their thresholds.

Lemma 1 (Threshold characterization):
For sequence \(A\), \(T(A)\) equals
\[
1+\max\{m\in\mathbb N:\ m\ \text{is not representable by finite sums of distinct
elements of }A\}
\]
when the maximum exists.
\[
\textit{Proof.}
\]
By definition of threshold as first point after which all integers are
representable.
\(\square\)
Lemma 2 (Immediate reversal criterion):
If for some \(k\), there exists \(x\) not representable by \((k+1)\)-powers but
every integer \(>x\) is representable by \((k+1)\)-powers, and \(x<T(n^k)\), then
\[
T(n^{k+1})\le x+1<T(n^k).
\]
\[
\textit{Proof.}
\]
First inequality from Lemma 1 for \(k+1\). Second is assumption.
\(\square\)
Lemma 3 (Reduction of EP-345):
To prove infinitely many reversals, it suffices to produce infinitely many \(k\)
satisfying Lemma 2's hypothesis.
\[
\textit{Proof.}
\]
Each such \(k\) yields one strict reversal by Lemma 2.
\(\square\)
