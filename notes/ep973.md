# EP-973

## Problem Statement
Does there exist a constant $C>1$ such that, for every $n\geq 2$, there exists a sequence $z_i\in \mathbb{C}$ with $z_1=1$ and $\lvert z_i\rvert \geq 1$ for all $1\leq i\leq n$ with\[\max_{2\leq k\leq n+1}\left\lvert \sum_{1\leq i\leq n}z_i^k\right\rvert < C^{-n}?\]

## Site Remarks
This is Problem 7.3 in \cite{Ha74}, where it is attributed to Erd\H{o}s.

Erd\H{o}s proved (as described on p.35 of \cite{Tu84b}) that such a sequence does exist with $\lvert z_i\rvert\leq 1$. Indeed, Erd\H{o}s' construction gives a value of $C\approx 1.32$.

In \cite{Er92f} (a different) Erd\H{o}s refines this analysis, proving that if\[M_2=\min_{z_j} \max_{2\leq k\leq n+1} \left\lvert \sum_{1\leq j\leq n}z_j^k\right\rvert,\]where the minimum is take over all $z_j\in \mathbb{C}$ with $\max \lvert z_j\rvert=1$, then\[(1.746)^{-n} < M_2 < (1.745)^{-n}.\]Tang notes in the comments that Theorem 6.1 of \cite{Tu84b} implies that, if $\lvert z_i\rvert \geq 1$ for all $i$, then\[\max_{2\leq k\leq n+1}\left\lvert \sum_{1\leq i\leq n}z_i^k\right\rvert \geq (2e)^{-(1+o(1))n}.\]See also [519].

  From Theorem 6.1 (the First Main Theorem) of [Tu84], if $\min_i|z_i|=1$ (hence $|z_i|\ge1$) and $g(\nu)=\sum_{i=1}^n b_i z_i^\nu$, then for any $m\ge0$\[

- 14:32 on 20 Sep 2025 - TerenceTao:
  Interestingly, as observed in Erdos's paper, the answer is completely different if one replaces the range $2 \leq k \leq n+1$ by $1 \leq k \leq n$. Here one has the example of the $(n+1)^{th}$ roots of unity (if $n+1$ is prime) to show that the max can be 1, but it was shown by Atkinson (https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF02066680) that it must be $\gg 1$ (and possibly even $\geq 1/2$)! Turan conjectures a lower bound of $1-o(1)$ as $n \to \infty$.
  
  It is not clear whether there is a similar construction showing that $1$ can be attained (or beaten) in the $2 \leq k \leq n+1$ case.
  
  EDIT: Here is a summary of Atkinson's argument. Suppose for contradiction that the power sums $s_k = \sum_{1 \leq i \leq n} z_i^k$ are all small for $1 \leq k \leq n$. The function $g(\theta) = -\sum_{k=1}^n \frac{s_k e(k\theta)}{k}$ is the Fourier expansion of $\sum_{1 \leq i \leq n} \log(1-z_i e(\theta))$ up to degree $n$, hence the Fourier expansion of $e^{g(\theta)}$ agrees with $\prod_{1 \leq i \leq n} (1-z_i e(\theta))$ up to degree $n$. If we set $z_1=1$, we conclude that at $\theta=0$, $e^{g(0)}$ is equal to the tail of its Fourier expansion after degree $n$; thus $e^{g(0)}$ is formally equal to the inner product of $e^{g(\theta)}$ and $\sum_{m=n+1}^\infty e(m\theta)$, or on integrating by parts of $e^{g(\theta)} g'(\theta)$ and $-\sum_{m=n+1}^\infty e(m\theta)/2\pi i -m$. Thus the inner product of $e^{g(\theta)-g(0)} g'(\theta)$ and $-\sum_{m=n+1}^\infty e(m\theta)/2\pi i m$ is equal to $1$. But using Plancherel's theorem to control the derivative of $g'$, one can show by standard estimates that this inner product is small if the $s_k$ are all small, giving the result.
  
  More informally: $e^{g(\theta)-g(0)}$ has significant presence at high frequencies, which is why $g'$ cannot have all of its Fourier coefficients small.

## Local Proof Attempts
Rigorous mean-square lower bound (all powers):
For fixed \(z_1,\dots,z_n\) on the unit circle,
\[
\liminf_{K\to\infty}\frac1K\sum_{k=1}^K |S_k|^2 \ge n.
\]
Proof:
Expand
\[
|S_k|^2=\sum_{i,j=1}^n (z_i\overline{z_j})^k.
\]
Averaging over \(k\le K\):
- diagonal terms \(i=j\) contribute exactly \(n\);
- off-diagonal terms with ratio \(\neq 1\) have Cesaro average tending to \(0\);
- off-diagonal terms with ratio \(=1\) (coincident points) contribute nonnegative amount.
So the asymptotic mean-square is at least \(n\).

Corollary:
\[
\sup_{k\ge 1}|S_k|\ge \sqrt n.
\]
Indeed, otherwise \(|S_k|^2<n\) for all \(k\), contradicting the averaged limit.

Relevance to EP-973:
This unconditional lower bound concerns all powers \(k\), not only the short window \(2\le k\le n+1\). So it does not settle the posed short-range extremal question, but it gives a rigorous obstruction against uniformly tiny power sums across all orders.
