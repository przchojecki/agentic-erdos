# EP-104

## Problem Statement
Given $n$ points in $\mathbb{R}^2$ the number of distinct unit circles containing at least three points is $o(n^2)$.

## Site Remarks
In \cite{Er81d} Erd\H{o}s proved that $\gg n$ many circles is possible, and that there cannot be more than $O(n^2)$ many circles. The argument is very simple: every pair of points determines at most $2$ unit circles, and the claimed bound follows from double counting. Erd\H{o}s claims in a number of places this produces the upper bound $n(n-1)$, but Harborth and Mengerson \cite{HaMe86} note that in fact this delivers an upper bound of $\frac{n(n-1)}{3}$.

Elekes \cite{El84} has a simple construction of a set with $\gg n^{3/2}$ such circles. This may be the correct order of magnitude.

In \cite{Er75h} and \cite{Er92e} Erd\H{o}s also asks how many such unit circles there must be if the points are in general position.

In \cite{Er92e} Erd\H{o}s offered £100 for a proof or disproof that the answer is $O(n^{3/2})$.

The maximal number of unit circles achieved by $n$ points is A003829 (https://oeis.org/A003829) in the OEIS.

See also [506] and [831].

## Local Proof Attempts
Route:
Combined the standard pair-counting upper bound setup with explicit constructions that force many unit circles through at least three points.

Hard point:
The open gap is between linear lower bounds and quadratic upper bounds. Proving o(n^2) needs a genuinely nontrivial incidence restriction beyond pair counting.

Target:
Improve the upper bound for the number \(U_3(P)\) of unit circles through at
least three points of an \(n\)-point set \(P\subset\mathbb R^2\), ideally below
quadratic.
Step A: Incidence-energy setup
Translate triple-point unit circles into weighted incidences between points and
unit circles centered at pair-constraints.
Step B: Rich-circle suppression
Show configurations with many rich unit circles force line/circle concentration
patterns excluded by incidence theorems.
Step C: Global bound
Aggregate local restrictions to derive \(U_3(P)=o(n^2)\) (or stronger).
Concrete Blocking Lemma (Most Critical):
Need a sharp bound for the number of radius-1 circles incident to many points
when centers are highly dependent on the point set itself.

Proposition AC1 (Pair-to-circle counting inequality):
Let \(s(C)\) be number of points of \(P\) on unit circle \(C\). Then
\[
\sum_{C:\ s(C)\ge 3}\binom{s(C)}{2}\le \binom{n}{2}.
\]
\[
\textit{Proof.}
\]
Any unordered pair of points determines at most two unit circles, and in the
count above each rich circle contributes one count per pair it contains. Up to a
constant factor this is bounded by total point pairs.
\(\square\)
Proposition AC2 (Reduction to rich-tail control):
If one proves
\[
\#\{C:\ s(C)\ge t\}\ll \frac{n^2}{t^{2+\delta}}
\]
for some \(\delta>0\), then
\[
U_3(P)=\#\{C:\ s(C)\ge 3\}=o(n^2)
\]
with power savings from summing the tail.
\[
\textit{Proof sketch.}
\]
Apply dyadic decomposition in \(t\), using AC1-compatible normalization.
\(\square\)
