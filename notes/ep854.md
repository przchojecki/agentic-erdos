# EP-854

## Problem Statement
Let $n_k$ denote the $k$th primorial, i.e. the product of the first $k$ primes.

If $1=a_1<a_2<\cdots a_{\phi(n_k)}=n_k-1$ is the sequence of integers coprime to $n_k$, then estimate the smallest even integer not of the form $a_{i+1}-a_i$. Are there\[\gg \max_i (a_{i+1}-a_i)\]many even integers of the form $a_{j+1}-a_j$?

## Site Remarks
This was asked by Erd\H{o}s in Oberwolfach (most likely in 1986). Clearly all differences $a_{i+1}-a_i$ are even. Erd\H{o}s first thought that (for large enough $k$) all even $t\leq \max(a_{i+1}-a_i)$ can be written as $t=a_{j+1}-a_j$ for some $j$, but in \cite{Ob1} writes 'perhaps this is false', and reports some computations of Lacampagne and Selfridge that this fails for $n_k=2\cdot 3\cdot 5\cdot 7\cdot 11\cdot 13$ which 'show some doubt on [his] conjecture', and says it could fail for all or infinitely many $k$.

In \cite{Ob1} he also asks about the set of $j$ for which $a_{j+1}-a_j=\max(a_{i+1}-a_i)$, and in particular asks for estimates on the number of such $j$ and the minimal value of such a $j$.

  $\textbf{Remark on different variant (not initial question)}$
  
  If we would consider all differences of the form $a_i-a_j$, the answer becomes very different.
  *if not excluding odd integers, the answer is 1 (this is also the case for the initial problem)
  *if we are restricting to even integers, it is $(1-o(1))n_k$
  
  If we denote $f(k)$ for the smallest positive even integer not of the form $a_i-a_j$,
  then the right but harder question would be to estimate how large $n_k-f(k)$ is.
  I could easily prove that this is $o(n_k)$ (e.g. bounded by $4n_{k-1}$), and with some work even that it is much smaller for large $k$.
  Nevertheless, the right answer here should be $n_k-f(k)=(2+o(1))p_k$.
  
  The lower bound for that is trivial.
  Note that the sequence starts with $a_1=1, a_2=p_{k+1}, a_3=p_{k+2} \ldots$ (till $p_k^2$ appears at some point)
  and $a_{\phi{(n_k)}+1-i}=n-a_i$.
  Let $r$ be an odd composite prime smaller than $2p_{k+1}$, then $n_k-(r-1)$ cannot be written as $a_i-a_j$, since $a_{\phi{(n_k)}-1}-a_2$<$n_k-(r+1)$ and $a_{\phi{(n_k)}}-a_i=a_{\phi{(n_k)}+1-i}-a_1$ cannot be $r-1$, since $r$ is not a prime (and it is smaller than $p_k^2$).
  
  The upper bound is not trivial (understatement)
  Assumption (stronger form Goldbach's conjecture):
  For an integer $q$>$0$, every even integer larger than some $2q+o(q)$ can be written as the sum of two primes larger than $q$.
  It even may well be that the $o(q)$ is polylog(q).
  
  Now take an even number (bounded by $n_k$) larger larger than $(2+o(1))p_{k}$,
  this can be written as the sum $p_r+p_s$ with $r,s$>$k$ by the above assumption.
  Now $p_r$ and $n_k-p_s$ are both relative prime with $n_k$ and thus of the form $a_i,a_j$.
  Now $a_j-a_i=n_k-(p_r+p_s).$
  
  From this we conclude.
  
  For completeness, hereby also the easy proof (which can be sharpened, but not up to the right form)
  By induction, for $k \ge 2$, and even $m$, does $m$ or $n_k-m$ appear as $a_i-a_j$. (base step $k=2,3$ trivial to check)
  
  Now going to $n_{k+1}$, take an even number $m$<$n_{k+1}-4n_k$.
  Let $m=r n_k + s.$
  Then $s$ or $n_k-s$ appeared before as a difference.
  If it is $n_k-s$, then write $n_k+a_i-a_j=s$.
  
  Now $((r+v)n_k+a_j,vn_k+a_i)$ or $((r+v+1)n_k+a_i,vn_k+a_j)$ is a solution for some $v \in \{0,1,2\}$ since for each linear combination, only one $v$ can lead to a multiple of $p_{k+1}.$
  So each such $m$ can be written as the difference of two numbers relative prime with $n_{k+1}$ smaller than $n_{k+1}.$
  
  Summary
  smallest even number not of the form $a_i-a_j$ is proven to be at most $n_k-4n_{k-1}=(1-o(1))n_k$
  and conjectured to be $n_k-(2+o(1))p_k$.

## Local Computation
Finite checks isolate a genuine counterexample to the older stronger conjecture and support the positive coverage direction.
- For \(k=6\), with \(P_6=2\cdot3\cdot5\cdot7\cdot11\cdot13=30030\), the reduced residues have maximum consecutive gap \(22\), and the even gap values occurring up to \(22\) are
\[
\{2,4,6,8,10,12,14,16,18,22\},
\]
so \(20\) is missing.
- Therefore the historical claim that every even value up to the maximum gap occurs is false.
- Additional finite scans of primorial levels up to \(k\le 9\) and constructive witness checks for many \((k,t)\) pairs support the positive side: in all tested edge cases with \(4\le k\le 30\), the value \(t=k\) (or \(k-1\) when \(k\) is odd) was represented.

## Local Proof Attempts
Two constructive results are established. First, for every fixed even \(t\ge 2\), one can choose congruences modulo the prime factors of \(P_k\) so that \(x\) and \(x+t\) are coprime to \(P_k\) while every interior point \(x+s\) with \(1\le s<t\) is forced to share a factor with \(P_k\); the Chinese remainder theorem then gives a represented gap \(t\) for all sufficiently large \(k\). Second, the same endpoint-interior forcing can be made uniform: for every \(k\ge 4\), every even \(t\le k\) occurs, and more generally every even \(t\le (2-\varepsilon)k\) occurs for all sufficiently large \(k\). As a corollary, the first missing even gap tends to infinity with \(k\). What remains open is the stronger quantitative comparison between the number of represented even gap sizes and the maximal gap.
