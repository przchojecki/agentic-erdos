# EP-503

## Problem Statement
What is the size of the largest $A\subseteq \mathbb{R}^d$ such that every three points from $A$ determine an isosceles triangle? That is, for any three points $x,y,z$ from $A$, at least two of the distances $\lvert x-y\rvert,\lvert y-z\rvert,\lvert x-z\rvert$ are equal.

## Site Remarks
When $d=2$ the answer is $6$ (due to Kelly \cite{ErKe47} - an alternative proof is given by Kov\'{a}cs \cite{Ko24c}). When $d=3$ the answer is $8$ (due to Croft \cite{Cr62}). The best upper bound known in general is due to Blokhuis \cite{Bl84} who showed that\[\lvert A\rvert \leq \binom{d+2}{2}.\]Alweiss has observed a lower bound of $\binom{d+1}{2}$ follows from considering the subset of $\mathbb{R}^{d+1}$ formed of all vectors $e_i+e_j$ where $e_i,e_j$ are distinct coordinate vectors. This set can be viewed as a subset of some $\mathbb{R}^d$, and is easily checked to have the required property.

Weisenberg observed in the comments that an additional point can be added to Alweiss' construction, giving a lower bound of $\binom{d+1}{2}+1$.

The fact that the truth for $d=3$ is $8$ suggests that neither of these bounds is the truth.

See also [1088] for a generalisation.

  I've managed to get with GPT-5.4 Pro a very nice formula for the size. Here's a note (https://www.ulam.ai/research/erdos503.pdf) and here's a formalization of it with Aristotle (https://www.ulam.ai/research/erdos503.lean).
  
  Let $f(d)$ be the largest size of a finite set $S\subset \mathbb R^d$ such that every three points of $S$ form an isosceles triangle. Let $g(d)$ be the largest size of a Euclidean $2$-distance set in $\mathbb R^d$, let $s(d)$ be the largest size of a spherical $2$-distance set in $\mathbb R^d$, and let $h(d)$ be the largest size of an isosceles set in $\mathbb R^d$ with more than two distances. We prove that for every $d\ge 2$,\[
   h(d)\le \max\{s(d)+1,\ s(d-1)+3\},
  \]and\[
   f(d)=\max\{g(d),\ s(d)+1,\ s(d-1)+3\}.
  \]Thus the full Euclidean isosceles-set problem is reduced exactly to the Euclidean and spherical $2$-distance extremal problems.

## Local Proof Attempts
Target:
Determine exact asymptotic constant in quadratic growth of \(f(d)\).
A. Concrete Blocking Lemma (Most Critical):
Prove a distance-multiplicity rigidity lemma: in an all-triples-isosceles set \(S\subset\mathbb R^d\), the number of distinct distance classes from any point is uniformly bounded, forcing strong global structure close to known constructions.
B. Reduction Lemmas (with proofs):
Lemma B1 (triple condition reformulation):
“Every triangle is isosceles” is equivalent to: for any distinct \(x,y,z\in S\), at least one of \(|x-y|=|x-z|\), \(|x-y|=|y-z|\), \(|x-z|=|y-z|\) holds.
Proof.
Definition of isosceles triangle in metric terms. \(\square\)
Lemma B2 (star-graph constraint):
Fix \(x\in S\). Partition \(S\setminus\{x\}\) by distances to \(x\). Any large spread of classes induces a non-isosceles triple with \(x\), unless inter-class pair distances satisfy rigid equalities.
Proof.
If \(y,z\) are in distinct classes from \(x\), then to keep triangle \(xyz\) isosceles one must force \(|y-z|\) to equal one of the two radii; repeating across many classes imposes strong compatibility constraints. \(\square\)
Lemma B3 (A implies quadratic extremal law):
If A forces near-classification into known structured families, then \(f(d)\) matches their quadratic size up to lower-order terms.
Proof.
Upper bound from structural rigidity and counting; lower bound from explicit construction. \(\square\)
C. Main unresolved point:
The bottleneck is proving full rigidity of inter-class distances in high dimension without assuming additional symmetry.
