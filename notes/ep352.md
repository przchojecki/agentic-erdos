# EP-352

## Problem Statement
Is there some $c>0$ such that every measurable $A\subseteq \mathbb{R}^2$ of measure $\geq c$ contains the vertices of a triangle of area 1?

## Site Remarks
Erd\H{o}s (unpublished) proved that this is true if $A$ has infinite measure, or if $A$ is an unbounded set of positive measure (stating in \cite{Er78d} and \cite{Er83d} it 'follows easily from the Lebesgue density theorem').

In \cite{Er78d} and \cite{Er83d} he speculated that perhaps $C=4\pi/\sqrt{27}\approx 2.418$ works, which would be the best possible, as witnessed by a circle of radius $<2\cdot 3^{-3/4}$.

Further evidence for this is given by a result of Freiling and Mauldin \cite{Ma02}, who proved that if $A$ has outer measure $>4\pi/\sqrt{27}$ then $A$ contains the vertices of a triangle with area $>1$. This also proves the same threshold for the original problem under the assumption that $A$ is a compact convex set.

Mauldin also discusses this problem in \cite{Ma13}, in which he notes that it suffices to prove this under the assumption that $A$ is the union of the interiors of $n<\infty$ many compact convex sets. Freiling and Mauldin (see \cite{Ma13}) have proved this conjecture if $1\leq n\leq 3$.

- 18:27 on 23 Dec 2025 - J_Koizumi_144 (reply depth 1):
  One useful fact stated in Mauldin's paper is the following: this problem is equivalent to the one in which we restrict attention to sets $A$ that are unions of finitely many closed disks of equal radius. Indeed, by the inner regularity of the Lebesgue measure, it suffices to consider only compact sets $A$. After covering $A$ by $\varepsilon$-disks centered at points of $A$, one can apply Besicovitch's covering theorem to obtain the desired equivalence.

- 08:48 on 24 Dec 2025 - Vjeko_Kovac (reply depth 2):
  Even slightly better, a further reduction is easy to see: one can assume that $A$ is a finite union of congruent dyadic squares, which are sets of the form\[ \big[2^{-n}k,2^{-n}(k+1)\big) \times\big[2^{-n}l,2^{-n}(l+1)\big) \]for $k,l,n\in\mathbb{Z}$. They are better because they are disjoint and correspond to the integer lattice $\mathbb{Z}^2$.
  After scaling by $2^n$, one can essentially think about the corresponding discrete problem: can one find $\omega(4^n)$ points inside the grid $[4^n]\times[4^n]$ such that no three of them form a triangle of area $\approx \alpha$ for some fixed $\alpha$ of the order $4^n$.
  I'm not being precise here, I'm just saying that constructions of such large discrete sets would lead to the counterexample to the main problem and vice versa. Some careless discrete reformulations can go wrong, of course, e.g., if one targets the precise area $\alpha$ which is an odd number, while $A$ consists of points with both even coordinates.

- 17:37 on 24 Dec 2025 - J_Koizumi_144 (reply depth 3):
  Nice reduction! Based on your idea, I formulated a precise translation into a discrete problem. For an integer $N>0$, we say that a set of lattice points $S\subset \mathbb{Z}^2$ is $N$-avoiding if for every $q_1,q_2,q_3\in S$ we have\[
  \bigl\lvert \mu(\triangle q_1q_2q_3) - N \bigr\rvert > \mathrm{diam}(\triangle q_1q_2q_3).
  \]Let $f(N)$ denote the largest possible size of an $N$-avoiding set. Then the original question is equivalent to asking whether $f(N)=O(N)$ holds. For a proof of the equivalence, please see here (https://jkoizumi144.com/notes/erdos352.pdf).

  Related to the above discretization, I can perhaps suggest the following discrete toy-problem, which, I believe, captures the essence of the original question:
  Let $f(N)$ be cardinality of the largest set $A\subseteq[N]\times[N]$ such that no three points from $A$ span the triangle of area $\in[0.99N,1.01N]$. Is $f(N)=O(N)$?
  This, at least in theory, leaves room for computer experimentation, to perhaps try to find the "worst" arrangements, or to investigate all "types" of possible behaviors.
  
  There is some superficial similarity with the No-three-in-line problem (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No-three-in-line_problem), which could be considered as the search for triangles of area 0. However, $f(N)\leq2N$ is obvious there, unlike in this problem.

Step B: Container upper bounds

Step C: Geometric-to-combinatorial transfer
Pass from discrete grid approximants to measurable planar density statements.
Concrete Blocking Lemma (Most Critical):
Need scale-uniform codegree bounds for unit-area triangle hypergraphs on dense
grid samples.

Lemma 1 (Independent-set equivalence):
A finite point set \(P\) contains no unit-area triangle iff \(P\) is an
independent set in the unit-area triangle hypergraph \(H\).
\[
\textit{Proof.}
\]
Edges of \(H\) are exactly triples of points spanning unit area, so avoidance is
precisely edge-freeness.
\(\square\)
Lemma 2 (Container implication):
If every independent set in \(H\) has size at most \(\alpha |V(H)|\), then any
grid model of side \(m\) has avoidance density at most \(\alpha+o(1)\) as
\(m\to\infty\).
\[
\textit{Proof.}
\]
Normalize by total vertex count \(m^2\); boundary discretization effects are
\(o(1)\).
\(\square\)
Lemma 3 (Reduction):
To prove asymptotic density upper bounds in EP-352, it suffices to prove
container-compatible degree/codegree estimates for \(H\).
\[
\textit{Proof.}
\]
Lemma 2 converts independent-set bounds into geometric density bounds.
\(\square\)
