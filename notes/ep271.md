# EP-271

## Problem Statement
Let $A(n)=\{a_0<a_1<\cdots\}$ be the sequence defined by $a_0=0$ and $a_1=n$, and for $k\geq 1$ define $a_{k+1}$ as the least positive integer such that there is no three-term arithmetic progression in $\{a_0,\ldots,a_{k+1}\}$.

Can the $a_k$ be explicitly determined? How fast do they grow?

## Site Remarks
It is easy to see that $A(1)$ is the set of integers which have no 2 in their base 3 expansion. Odlyzko and Stanley \cite{OdSt78} have found similar characterisations are known for $A(3^k)$ and $A(2\cdot 3^k)$ for any $k\geq 0$ and conjectured in general that such a sequence always eventually either satisfies\[a_k\asymp k^{\log_23}\]or\[a_k \asymp \frac{k^2}{\log k}.\]There is no known sequence which satisfies the second growth rate, but Lindhurst \cite{Li90} gives data which suggests that $A(4)$ has such growth ($A(4)$ is given as A005487 (https://oeis.org/A005487) in the OEIS).

Moy \cite{Mo11} has proved that, for all such sequences, for all $\epsilon>0$, $a_k\leq (\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon)k^2$ for all sufficiently large $k$. van Doorn and Sothanaphan have noted in the comment section that Moy's proof can be upgraded to give a fully explicit result of\[a_k\leq \frac{(k-1)(k+2)}{2}+n\]for all $k\geq 0$.

In general, sequences which begin with some initial segment and thereafter are continued in a greedy fashion to avoid three-term arithmetic progressions are known as Stanley sequences.

  I haven't fully checked the paper by Moy (even though it's short and elementary), but we can actually eliminate the $\epsilon$ in their result and make the 'sufficiently large $k$' explicit.
  
  To see this, asssume $k > 2n$ and define $x = \frac{1}{2}k^2$. Now assume by contradiction $a_k > x$, or equivalently in the notation of Moy, assume $S(\{n\}, x) \le k-1$. Then their Lemma 2.4 gives $x \le \frac{1}{2}k(k-1) + n = x - \frac{1}{2}k + n < x$; contradiction. We therefore deduce $S(\{n\}, x) \ge k$. Translated back into the notation of this page, this gives $a_k \le \frac{1}{2}k^2$ for all $k > 2n$.

  Slight correction: the $k$th element in this page’s notation is $a_{k-1}$, so actually $a_{k-1} \leq k^2/2$ for $k \geq 2n+1$, or $a_k \leq (k+1)^2/2$ for $k \geq 2n$.
  
  But actually we can “optimize” to the max as follows.
  
  In the proof of [Moy]’s Lemma 2.2, since $n = 2a_j - a_i \geq a_j + 1$, we can replace “less than or equal to $x$” with “less than or equal to $x-1$”. This means the statement of Lemma 2.2 can replace $S(A,x)$ with $S(A,x-1)$.
  
  In the statement of Lemma 2.3, we can replace $\max A$ with $(\max A - |A| + 1)$ by simple counting.
  
  Then, in Lemma 2.4, if we assume further that $x$ is integer, we can get rid of the bound $\lfloor x\rfloor + 1 \geq x$. Combined with the improved Lemma 2.2 and Lemma 2.3, the statement of Lemma 2.4 changes to:
  $$ x \leq S(A,x-1)(S(A,x-1) - 1)/2 + S(A,x) + \max A - |A| $$
  for integer $x$.
  
  Now set $x = a_k$. Recall Moy’s notation: $S(A,y) = \# \{ a_i \leq y \}$. Here $A = \{0, n\}$. We arrive at the bound
  $$ a_k \leq k(k-1)/2 + (k+1) + n - 2. $$
  So unconditionally:
  $$ a_k \leq (k-1)(k+2)/2 + n. $$
  
  (Sanity check: $a_0 \leq n-1$, $a_1 \leq n$, $a_2 \leq n+2$ all make sense and can be tight if $n = 1$.)

Lemma 1 (Strict increase and unboundedness):
Any EP-271 greedy sequence \((a_n)\) is strictly increasing and unbounded.
\[
\textit{Proof.}
\]
At step \(n+1\), by construction \(a_{n+1}\) is chosen among integers larger than
the previous terms and avoiding finitely many forbidden values. Thus
\(a_{n+1}>a_n\). A strictly increasing sequence of integers is unbounded.
\(\square\)
Lemma 2 (Finite-memory admissibility):
For each step \(n\), admissibility of a candidate \(x\) depends only on finitely
many constraints derived from \(\{a_1,\dots,a_n\}\). Hence the forbidden set at
step \(n\) is finite.
\[
\textit{Proof.}
\]
Each defining constraint involves finitely many previous indices; union over
finitely many index tuples up to \(n\) is finite.
\(\square\)
Lemma 3 (Existence of next term):
Given Lemma 2, \(a_{n+1}\) exists for every \(n\).
\[
\textit{Proof.}
\]
Among integers \(>a_n\), only finitely many are forbidden at step \(n\). Hence at
least one admissible integer exists; choose the least (greedy rule).
\(\square\)
Lemma 4 (Reduction to exponent uniqueness):
If there exist constants \(c_1,c_2,\alpha>1\) such that for all large \(n\),
\[
c_1 n^\alpha\le a_n\le c_2 n^\alpha,
\]
then growth is polynomial of exponent \(\alpha\). Therefore the open part is
uniqueness/identification of \(\alpha\) by seed.
\[
\textit{Proof.}
\]
The inequalities are exactly \(a_n=\Theta(n^\alpha)\).
\(\square\)
