# EP-835

## Problem Statement
Does there exist a $k>2$ such that the $k$-sized subsets of $\{1,\ldots,2k\}$ can be coloured with $k+1$ colours such that for every $A\subset \{1,\ldots,2k\}$ with $\lvert A\rvert=k+1$ all $k+1$ colours appear among the $k$-sized subsets of $A$?

## Site Remarks
A problem of Erd\H{o}s and Rosenfeld. This is trivially possible for $k=2$. They were not sure about $k=6$.

This is equivalent to asking whether there exists $k>2$ such that the chromatic number of the Johnson graph (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johnson_graph) $J(2k,k)$ is $k+1$ (it is always at least $k+1$ and at most $2k$). The chromatic numbers listed at this website (https://www.win.tue.nl/~aeb/graphs/Johnson.html) show that this is false for $3\leq k\leq 8$.

Ma and Tang have proved (https://github.com/QuanyuTang/erdos-problem-835/blob/main/On_Problem_835.pdf) that the chromatic number of $J(2k,k)$ is $>k+1$ for all $k>2$ not of the form $p-1$ for prime $p$.

  It's annoying that you can't handle the case $k+1$ prime. Looking at the reason it's known for $k=4$, for example, this follows because the chromatic number of $J(6,3)$ is $6$ and hence the chromatic number of $J(7,4)\cong J(7,3)$ is also at least $6$, and so the chromatic number of $J(8,4)$ is also at least $6$. Perhaps a similar monotonicity trick would handle $k+1$ prime (e.g. if you can prove a lower bound of $k+3$ for all $k\neq p-1$ this would imply a lower bound of $k+2$ for all $k$).

- 10:00 on 26 Jan 2026 - YaelDillies (reply depth 1):
  AlphaProof (on 2025-12-26) found a proof that $J(18, 9) \ne 10$, which was the first result we marked as open in formal-conjectures (albeit it was in fact not, see below).
  
  Then we realised this proof could easily be adapted to work for all odd $k$ (on 2025-12-27) and Lean agreed. We even thought it could be generalised to all $k$ such that $k + 1$ is composite, but did not pursue this further as we simultaneously realised that Johnson's bound (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johnson_bound) along with the independence bound on the chromatic number of a graph gives the same value (or even better sometimes).
  
  Concretely, Johnson proved that the independence number of $J(n, k)$ is at most $A(n, 4, k)$, which is recursively defined by $A(n, 4, 1) = 1$ and $A(n, 4, k) = \lfloor \frac nk A(n - 1, 4, k - 1)\rfloor$ for $k > 1$. The lower bound on the chromatic number is then $\lceil \binom{2k}k/ A(2k, 4, k)\rceil$, which is at least $k + 2$ precisely when $k + 1$ is composite.
  
  We thought to mention here that the result is in fact not novel, although it does indeed seem no one in the coding theory literature bothered to evaluate $A(2k, 4, k)$ in relation to this problem.
  
  The original Alphaproof proof is here (https://github.com/google-deepmind/formal-conjectures/commit/efbe1f0237ea2bbcac25b0eb338f7245f35b202a), the human-powered generalisation is there (https://github.com/google-deepmind/formal-conjectures/commit/046b8b893873fd83f2e1b979a8b6ffb702a922d1), and the current state of the formal proof is in this PR to formal-conjectures (https://github.com/google-deepmind/formal-conjectures/pull/1428).

- 09:10 on 31 Dec 2025 - eigensolver:
  The independence number of a Johnson graph $\alpha(J(n,k))$ is equal to the maximum number of codewords $A(n,4,k)$ in a binary constant-weight code of length $n$, weight $k$, and minimum distance $\ge 4$, which gives a lower bound for the chromatic number:
  $$\chi (J(2k,k)) ≥ \dfrac{\binom{2k}{k}}{A(2k,4,k)}. $$
  According to the bound table for $A(n,4,w)$ listed here (https://codes.se/bounds/cw.html), it can be concluded that the original proposition does not hold for $3\le k\le 14$. I also wrote a brute force program that extends this table using Theorem 9 from [AgVaZe00] (https://doi.org/10.1109/18.887851), verifying that such coloring does not exist for $k\le 500$ (except for $k = 16, 112, 256, 268$, where the lower bound is slightly weaker than $k+1$, with a difference ranging from $0$ to $1.14\times10^{-13}$). I believe that by slightly improving the recursive upper bound for $A(2k,4,k)$, it is hopeful to provide a complete proof.
  
  Edit: Sorry for the earlier mistake in my code, it suffered from precision issues. After correcting them, I found that it indeed fails to handle the vast majority of cases where $k+1$ is prime, just as in the difficulty encountered by Tang. Moreover, [Jor25] (https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.15019) shows that this is already the best possible outcome obtainable from the recursive Johnson bound for $A(n, 4, w)$ (see 4.5 and 5.5). To fully resolve this problem, one may need to investigate the nonexistence of (a large set of) Steiner system $S(2k, k, k-1)$.
