# EP-335

## Problem Statement
Let $d(A)$ denote the density of $A\subseteq \mathbb{N}$. Characterise those $A,B\subseteq \mathbb{N}$ with positive density such that\[d(A+B)=d(A)+d(B).\]

## Site Remarks
One way this can happen is if there exists $\theta>0$ such that\[A=\{ n>0 : \{ n\theta\} \in X_A\}\textrm{ and }B=\{ n>0 : \{n\theta\} \in X_B\}\]where $\{x\}$ denotes the fractional part of $x$ and $X_A,X_B\subseteq \mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$ are such that $\mu(X_A+X_B)=\mu(X_A)+\mu(X_B)$. Are all possible $A$ and $B$ generated in a similar way (using other groups)?

This has been partially resolved by Ackelsburg and Richter \cite{AcRi26}, under the additional assumption that one of the sets meets every residue class in $\mathbb{N}$. Roughly speaking, if $d(A)>0$ and $d(A+B)=d(A)+d(B)<1$ and $B$ meets every residue class then either both $A$ and $B$ arise from a rotation on the product of the circle group with a finite cyclic group, or $A$ is in a single residue class modulo some $h$ and $B$ 'looks like' the union of all but one residue class modulo $h$.

A full characterisation without the assumption that one of the sets meets every residue class seems hopeless; for example, taking $A=B$ to be a random subset of the even integers with density $1/4$ we have $d(A+A)=1/2$.

- 05:08 on 15 Apr 2026 - eackelsberg (reply depth 1):
  Thanks for linking our paper so quickly! Here's a brief summary of our main result with some comments on the assumption about $B$ meeting every residue class in $\mathbb{N}$.
  
  The general case does not seem to have any simple characterization using rotations on groups. The problem is that modular obstructions can get in the way. For example, if $A$ is a random subset of the even numbers with density $1/4$, then $A+A$ will be almost all of the even numbers and have density $1/2$. This provides an example of a highly non-structured set satisfying $d(A+A) = 2d(A)$.
  
  We bypass such situations by assuming that one of the sets (we take it to be the set $B$) completely avoids modular obstructions. Our main result says that under this assumption that $B$ meets every residue class in $\mathbb{N}$ and $d(A) + d(B) < 1$, one of two things happens:
  
  - the sets $A$ and $B$ arise from a rotation on the product of the circle group with a finite cyclic group, or
  - $A$ is contained in a single residue class mod $h$ for some $h$ and B “looks like” the union of all but one residue classes mod $h$
  
  The precise statement (with a rigorous meaning of what it means for $B$ to “look like” the union of all but one residue classes mod $h$) can be found as Theorem 1.4 in our paper.

## Local Computation
Cyclic-group experiments comparing random sets with interval-model sets showed a sharp structural split.
Interval-model pairs hit the lower-bound equality profile at nearly \(100\%\) rate in the tested scans, while random pairs produced no exact hits and sat well above the minimal sumset size.
This finite evidence supports the interval/rotation-type picture behind the equality case.
