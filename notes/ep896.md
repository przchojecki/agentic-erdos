# EP-896

## Problem Statement
Estimate the maximum of $F(A,B)$ as $A,B$ range over all subsets of $\{1,\ldots,N\}$, where $F(A,B)$ counts the number of $m$ such that $m=ab$ has exactly one solution (with $a\in A$ and $b\in B$).

## Site Remarks
The order of magnitude of $F(A,B)$ is now known:\[F(A,B)\asymp \frac{N^2}{(\log N)^\delta(\log\log N)^{3/2}}\]where $\delta=1-\frac{1+\log\log 2}{\log 2}\approx 0.086$. The upper bound is an immediate consequence of the bound on the size of $\{1,\ldots,N\}\cdot\{1,\ldots,N\}$ given by Ford \cite{Fo08}.

The lower bound was proved (https://www.ulam.ai/research/erdos896.pdf) by GPT-5.5 Pro (prompted by Chojecki), using a similar result from \cite{Fo08}; the proof is sketched in the comments.

See also [490].

- 09:30 on 02 May 2026 - Thomas Bloom (reply depth 1):
  Thanks! As you say, the upper bound is immediate from Ford's work as noted by Wouter. The lower bound here is a neat generalisation of Szemerédi's lower bound construction, boosting $A$ from just a set of large primes to the set of integers divisible by a large prime. The real work here is done by Ford's deep work on integers with a unique divisor in a dyadic interval.
  
  Here is a cleaned up and simplified description of the argument.
  
  The input from Ford's seminal paper (https://arxiv.org/abs/math/0401223) is that, if both $y$ and $N$ are large enough, and $y\leq N^{1/3}$, then the number of $n\leq Ny$ which have exactly one divisor in $(y,2y]$ is\[\gg \frac{Ny}{(\log y)^\delta(\log\log y)^{3/2}}=N\Phi(y),\]say. (This is an immediate consequence of combining Corollary 2 and Theorem 4 of Ford's paper.)
  
  Let $P$ be a random set of primes in $(N^{2/3},cN]$, where $c$ is some small constant, each included with probability $1/2$, and let $A$ be the set of all integers in $(N/2,N]$ divisible by at least one (and so exactly one) prime in $P$, and $B$ be the set of all integers in $[1,N]$ divisible by no prime in $P$.
  
  Now let $y_p=N/2p$ and $G_p$ be the set of all $n\leq Ny_p$ with exactly one divisor in $(y_p,2y_p]$, and let $G_p'\subseteq G_p$ be the subset of integers which are not divisible by any $q\in P$.
  
  If $d$ is this unique divisor, then note that $n/d\leq N$, and therefore $n/d\in B$. It follows that each $p\in P$ and $n\in G_p'$ contributes $1$ to $F(A,B)$, since $(pd)\cdot (n/d)$ is the unique representation of $pn$ as a product $ab$. (To see this, note that $d<N^{1/3}$, so if $pn=(qd')b$ for some $q\in P$ and $b\in B$ then $q=p$, and then also $d'=d$, since $n$ has a unique divisor in $(N/2p,N/p]$).
  
  Therefore\[F(A,B) \gg \sum_{p\in P}\lvert G_p'\rvert.\]Now since any $n\leq N$ can have at most one prime divisor in $(N^{2/3},N]$, which is discarded with probability $1/2$, for any prime $p$ we have $\mathbb{E} \lvert G_p'\rvert \gg \lvert G_p\rvert\gg N\Phi(y_p)$, and so (since those $n\in G_p$ which are divisible by $p$ itself are a negligible proportion, and choosing suitable random choice of $P$ by the pigeonhole principle)\[F(A,B) \gg \sum_{N^{2/3}<p\leq cN}\lvert G_p\rvert\gg N \sum_{N^{2/3}<p\leq cN}\Phi(y_p).\]By standard Mertens-type estimates this right-hand side is $\gg N\Phi(N)$ as desired.

- 23:17 on 23 Nov 2025 - Woett:
  With $\delta = 1 - \frac{1 + \log \log 2}{\log 2} \approx 0.086$ we have $$\frac{\big(1+o(1)\big)N^2}{\log N} \le \max_{A,B \subseteq \{1, 2, \ldots, N\}} F(A, B) \ll \frac{N^2}{\log^{\delta} N (\log \log N)^{3/2}}.$$The upper bound follows from the determination of the number of elements in the multiplication table $\{1, 2, \ldots, N\} \times \{1, 2, \ldots, N\}$ by Ford.
  
  For the lower bound we can use a construction Szemerédi gives in this (https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/0022314X76900032) paper (the one where he solved #490 (https://www.erdosproblems.com/490)): set $A$ to be the set of primes in $\left[\frac{N}{\log N}, N\right]$ and choose $B$ to be the subset of integers in $\{1, 2, \ldots, N\}$ which are not divisible by any $a \in A$. Then $|A| = \frac{\big(1+o(1)\big)N}{\log N}$ by the prime number theorem, $|B| = \big(1+o(1)\big)N$, and every element $m \in AB$ has a unique representation.
  
  For what it's worth, one can replace $\log N$ in the interval $\left[\frac{N}{\log N}, N\right]$ defining $A$, by any function $f(N)$ that goes to infinity with $N$ slower than $N^{\epsilon}$ for all $\epsilon > 0$.
  
  Erdős suggests in [Er72] that the ideas in the aforementioned paper by Szemerédi can perhaps be used to attack this problem as well.

## Local Computation
- Exact exhaustive optimization for small $N\in\{6,7,8,9,10\}$ by checking all pairs $(A,B)$.
- Deep constructive scans for larger $N\in\{1000,2000,4000,8000,12000\}$ using two families:
 - $A=[1,\lfloor N/2\rfloor]$, $B=$ primes in $(N/2,N]$.
 - $A=[1,N]$, $B=$ primes in $(N/2,N]$.
- Recorded unique-product counts, collision bins, and normalized ratio against $N^2/\log N$.

- For $A=[1,\lfloor N/2\rfloor]$, $B$ as above, collision bins are exactly $0$ in all tested deep cases, giving fully unique products in this construction.
- At $N=12000$ this construction gives $F=3{,}930{,}000$ and
$$
\frac{F}{N^2/\log N}\approx 0.25634.
$$
- Using full $A=[1,N]$ increases raw count but introduces collisions; at $N=12000$:
 - unique count $7{,}431{,}630$,
 - collision bins $214{,}185$,
 - normalized ratio $\approx 0.48474$.
- Small-$N$ exact maxima (up to $N=10$) provide calibration examples but are too small to determine asymptotic constants.

## Local Proof Attempts
The exact small-\(N\) optimization and the larger constructive families both show that unique-product counts on the scale \(N^2/\log N\) are genuinely attainable. The prime-tail-type constructions are especially clean because one family gives zero collisions in the tested range, but the true leading constant and sharp exponent remain open.
