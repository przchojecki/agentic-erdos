# EP-420

## Problem Statement
If $\tau(n)$ counts the number of divisors of $n$ then let\[F(f,n)=\frac{\tau((n+\lfloor f(n)\rfloor)!)}{\tau(n!)}.\]Is it true that\[\lim_{n\to \infty}F((\log n)^C,n)=\infty\]for large $C$?

Is it true that $F(\log n,n)$ is everywhere dense in $(1,\infty)$?

More generally, if $f(n)\leq \log n$ is a monotonic function such that $f(n)\to \infty$ as $n\to \infty$, then is $F(f,n)$ everywhere dense?

## Site Remarks
Erd\H{o}s and Graham write that it is easy to show that $\lim F(n^{1/2},n)=\infty$, and in fact the $n^{1/2}$ can be replaced by $n^{1/2-c}$ for some small constant $c>0$.

Erd\H{o}s, Graham, Ivi\'{c}, and Pomerance \cite{EGIP96} have proved that\[\liminf F(c\log n, n) = 1\]for any $c>0$, and\[\lim F(n^{4/9},n)=\infty.\](The exponent $4/9$ can be improved slightly.) They also prove that, if $f(n)=o((\log n)^2)$, then for almost all $n$\[F(f,n)\sim 1.\]van Doorn notes in the comments that the existence of infinitely many bounded prime gaps implies\[\limsup_{n\to \infty}F(g(n),n)=\infty\]for any $g(n)\to \infty$, and that Cram\'{e}r's conjecture implies\[\lim F(g(n)(\log n)^2, n)=\infty\]for any $g(n)\to \infty$>

  In [EGIP96] (also mentioned in #419 (https://www.erdosproblems.com/419)), the authors prove $\liminf_{n \rightarrow \infty} F(c \log n, n) \le 2$ for any constant $c > 0$, using ideas used in bounds on large prime gaps (which means that this result can perhaps be slightly improved by the resolution of #4 (https://www.erdosproblems.com/4), although I haven't checked), while $\liminf_{n \rightarrow \infty} F(n^{4/9}, n) \ge 2$. In fact, their results/proofs give $\liminf_{n \rightarrow \infty} F(c \log n, n) = 1$ and $\lim_{n \rightarrow \infty} F(n^{4/9}, n) = \infty$ respectively.
  
  This exponent $4/9$ can certainly be improved a little, but I'm not sure by how much. So as far as I can tell, $f(n) = n^{4/9}$ is the slowest growing function for which it is known that $F(f, n)$ goes to infinity with $n$. And for those interested, let me quickly say a few words on the idea behind the proof of this result.
  
  By Lemma $1$ in their paper, the ratio $\frac{\tau(n!)}{\tau((n-1)!)}$ is well-approximated by $1 + \frac{S(n)}{n}$, where $S(n)$ is the sum of the prime factors of $n$ (counted with multiplicity). Hence, to determine $F(f, n)$ it is more or less sufficient to determine the value of $\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{f(n)} S(n+i)$. In particular, if you have sufficiently many $n+i \in (n, n + f(n))$ which are divisible by large primes, then those $S(n+i)$ will also be large, and thereby so will $F(f, n)$ be.
  
  All in all, proving bounds on the number of integers in short intervals that have a large prime factor, captures the essential difficulty of this problem.
  
  Note by the way that $F(1, n) \le 2$ with equality if, and only if, $n+1$ is prime, so that $\lim_{n \rightarrow \infty} F(g(n) \log^2 n, n) = \infty$ follows from Cramér's conjecture for any function $g(n)$ that goes to infinity with $n$. Unconditionally, for such functions $g(n)$ we have $\limsup_{n \rightarrow \infty} F(g(n), n) = \infty$, by results on bounded prime gaps.

## Local Computation
Route:
Computed finite profiles of
F(f,n)=tau((n+floor(f(n)))!)/tau(n!)
for f=(log n)^C using logarithmic divisor-count ratios.

 - for sampled n, log F((log n)^1,n) fluctuates near small positive values;
 - higher exponents (C=2,3) give very large growth in the sampled range.

## Local Proof Attempts
Hard point:
The open regime is exactly around logarithmic scales where F can be very close to 1 infinitely often, making monotone-growth proofs delicate.

Target:
Resolve logarithmic-scale behavior for
\[
F(f,n)=\frac{\tau((n+\lfloor f(n)\rfloor)!)}{\tau(n!)}
\]
when \(f(n)\) is near \((\log n)^C\), including dense-value / liminf questions.
Step A: Prime-Sum Expansion at Log Scale
Derive precise asymptotic expansion for \(\log F(f,n)\) in terms of weighted
prime counts over \((n,n+f(n)]\), with error uniform for \(f(n)\le (\log n)^C\).
Step B: Oscillation Control
Show expansion has both positive and near-zero phases infinitely often (or
exclude one of them), via distribution of prime powers around \(n\).
Step C: Dense-Value/Liminf Resolution
Translate Step B to final statements on whether \(F(f,n)\) is frequently near 1
or forced away from 1 for given \(C\).

\]
at logarithmic \(f(n)\)-scales with power-saving error.
