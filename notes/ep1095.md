# EP-1095

## Problem Statement
Let $g(k)>k+1$ be the smallest $n$ such that all prime factors of $\binom{n}{k}$ are $>k$. Estimate $g(k)$.

## Site Remarks
A question of Ecklund, Erd\H{o}s, and Selfridge \cite{EES74}, who proved\[k^{1+c}<g(k)\leq \exp((1+o(1))k)\]for some constant $c>0$, and conjectured $g(k)<L_k=[1,\ldots,k]$, the least common multiple of all integers $\leq k$, for all large $k$. In \cite{EES74} they further conjecture that\[\limsup \frac{g(k+1)}{g(k)}=\infty\]and\[\liminf \frac{g(k+1)}{g(k)}=0.\]The lower bound was improved by Erd\H{o}s, Lacampagne, and Selfridge \cite{ELS93} and Granville and Ramar\'{e} \cite{GrRa96}. The current record is\[g(k) \gg \exp(c(\log k)^2)\]for some $c>0$, due to Konyagin \cite{Ko99b}.

Erd\H{o}s, Lacampagne, and Selfridge \cite{ELS93} write 'it is clear to every right-thinking person' that $g(k)\geq\exp(c\frac{k}{\log k})$ for some constant $c>0$.

Sorenson, Sorenson, and Webster \cite{SSW20} give heuristic evidence that\[\log g(k) \asymp \frac{k}{\log k}.\]See also [1094].

  Sorenson, Sorenson, and Webster in An algorithm and estimates for the Erdős–Selfridge function (2020) (https://msp.org/obs/2020/4-1/obs-v4-n1-p23-p.pdf) define the same function $g(k)$ and introduce an approximating function $\tilde g(k)$ based on counting admissible residues.
  They prove\[
   0.530684 + o(1) \le \frac{\log \tilde g(k)}{k/\log k} \le 1 + o(1),
  \]so in particular\[
   \log \tilde g(k) = \Theta\left(\frac{k}{\log k}\right).
  \]Under a uniform distribution heuristic of theirs, they show that\[
   \log g(k) = \log \tilde g(k) + O(\log k),
  \]which implies that $g(k)$ is, with high probability, within a polynomial factor of $\tilde g(k)$. Consequently, they heuristically expect $\log g(k) = \Theta\left(k/\log k\right)$, equivalently\[
   g(k) = \exp\left(\Theta\left(\frac{k}{\log k}\right)\right),
  \]a growth rate that is larger than Konyagin’s proven lower bound $g(k) \gg \exp\left(c(\log k)^2\right)$. However, this faster $\exp\left(\Theta(k/\log k)\right)$ growth for $g(k)$ is only supported by their heuristic and has not been proved unconditionally.

## Local Computation
- exact search using Lucas-theorem divisibility tests:
 - for each prime $p\le k$, test whether $p\mid\binom{n}{k}$ via base-$p$ digit comparison,
 - accept the first $n$ where all such tests are nondivisibility.
- This is exact for all found values within the chosen cap.

Deep run (DEPTH=4) used:
- $K_{\max}=90$,
- search cap $N_{\max}=2{,}500{,}000$,
- per-$k$ budget 14s.
Workload scale:
- about $121{,}782{,}980$ tested $n$-values,
- about $137{,}642{,}288$ Lucas prime checks.
Results summary:
- found exact $g(k)$ values for 42 values of $k\le 90$,
- unresolved within cap for 47 values,
- unresolved list starts at $k=41$ and includes most larger $k$ values in this window.
Observed finite profile remains highly irregular in $k$, consistent with known oscillatory heuristics.

## Local Proof Attempts
Target:
Estimate growth of \(g(k)\), the first \(n>k+1\) with all prime divisors of \(\binom nk\) exceeding \(k\).
A. Concrete Blocking Lemma (Most Critical):
Prove that for most \(k\), there is \(n\le \exp(Ck/\log k)\) such that
\[
p\nmid\binom nk\quad\forall p\le k.
\]
This would establish a near-heuristic upper envelope for \(g(k)\).
B. Reduction Lemmas (with proofs):
Lemma B1 (prime-threshold equivalence):
\[
g(k)=\min\{n>k+1:\ p\nmid\binom nk\ \forall p\le k\}.
\]
Proof.
All prime factors of \(\binom nk\) being \(>k\) is equivalent to nondivisibility by every prime \(p\le k\). \(\square\)
Lemma B2 (Lucas digit constraint):
For prime \(p\le k\),
\[
p\nmid\binom nk
\iff
\text{every base-}p\text{ digit of }k \le \text{corresponding digit of }n.
\]
Proof.
Directly Lucas theorem mod \(p\). \(\square\)
Lemma B3 (CRT digit-synthesis strategy):
If one can choose \(n\) whose base-\(p\) digits dominate those of \(k\) simultaneously for all \(p\le k\), then \(n\) is admissible and yields an upper bound for \(g(k)\).
Proof.
Apply Lemma B2 for each \(p\le k\); all conditions together imply the threshold property by Lemma B1. \(\square\)
C. Main unresolved point:
The obstruction is simultaneous control of digit-dominance constraints across many small primes without making \(n\) too large.
