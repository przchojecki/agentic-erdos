# EP-276

## Problem Statement
Is there an infinite Lucas sequence $a_0,a_1,\ldots$ where $a_{n+2}=a_{n+1}+a_n$ for $n\geq 0$ such that all $a_k$ are composite, and yet no integer has a common factor with every term of the sequence?

## Site Remarks
Whether such a composite Lucas sequence even exists was open for a while, but using covering systems Graham \cite{Gr64} showed that\[a_0 = 1786772701928802632268715130455793\]and\[a_1 = 1059683225053915111058165141686995\]generate such a sequence. This problem asks whether one can have a composite Lucas sequence without 'an underlying system of covering congruences responsible'.

This problem has been 'conjecturally solved' by Ismailescu and Son \cite{IsSo14}, in that they provide an explicit infinite Lucas sequence in which all the terms are composite, and believe that no covering system is responsible for this. See the comment by van Doorn below for more details.

See also [1113] for another problem in which the question is whether covering systems are always responsible.

- 13:09 on 24 Nov 2025 - Woett:
  The smallest known pair of starting values for which a Graham-type Lucas sequence exists, was gradually brought down by Knuth, Wilf, Nicol and Vsemirnov, with the latter holding the current record with $a_0 = 106276436867$ and $a_1 = 35256392432$ (despite Erdős and Graham writing 'it would be very surprising if a pair existed with both components less than $10^{20}$' in [ErGr80]!). So these starting values lead to a Lucas sequence which, thanks to a covering system, only contains composite values. Fun fact: in what feels like an hommage to Graham, the papers by Knuth, Nicol and Vsemirnov all have almost the same title A (new) Fibonacci-like sequence of composite numbers, which was also the title of Graham's original paper. Wilf's example can be found on the OEIS as A083216 (https://oeis.org/A083216).
  
  As for a Lucas sequence of composite integers without an underlying covering system (i.e. the question at hand), this was conjecturally solved by Ismailescu and Son in this (https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/journals/JIS/VOL17/Ismailescu/ism8.pdf) paper (appropriately titled A new kind of Fibonacci-like sequence of composite numbers), where you can find references to the aforementioned papers as well.
  
  With $p = 1$, $q$ a $129$-digit monstrosity (see Theorem 3 on page 6 of their paper), $a_0 = p^2 + q^2$, $a_1 = 2pq + q^2$ and $a_{n+2} = a_{n+1} + a_n$ for all $n \ge 0$, Ismailescu and Son prove the following:
  
  1) There exists a positive integer $m$ (for which all its prime factors are smaller than $a_0$) such that $\gcd(m, a_{2n}) > 1$ for all $n \ge 0$. That is, all even-indexed terms are composite thanks to a covering system.
  2) For all $n \ge 0$ we have $a_{2n+1} = (pF_n + qF_{n+1})(pL_n + qL_{n+1})$ with $F_n$ and $L_n$ the Fibonacci and Lucas numbers respectively. In particular, all odd-indexed terms are composite too.
  
  Now, as for these odd-indexed terms, there seems to be no reason to believe that a covering system is responsible here as well. However, they are not actually able to prove the non-existence of an $m$ such that $\gcd(m, a_{2n+1}) > 1$ holds for all $n \ge 0$. So that is why one could say that this problem is conjecturally solved.
  
  I do wonder if it's possible to use the ideas in Hough's theorem and follow-up work, to reduce deciding whether a covering system is responsible for the odd-indexed terms in the above example, to a finite computation.
  
  PS. Due to Dogmachine's comment the assumption on the coprimality of $a_0$ and $a_1$ was removed. However, this assumption is of course important for Graham's example.

## Local Proof Attempts
Target:
Construct (or rule out) Lucas-type sequences with all terms composite while
avoiding a trivial global common-factor mechanism.
Step A: Covering-congruence architecture
Assign congruence classes of indices forcing divisibility by selected primes for
each term \(u_n\).
Step B: Primitive-prime management
Control emergence of primitive prime divisors so covering remains valid at all
indices.
Step C: Global gcd collapse check
Verify forced divisibility does not imply a fixed common divisor across all terms.
Concrete Blocking Lemma (Most Critical):
Need a compatibility lemma between covering sets of indices and Lucas
recurrence residues ensuring every index is covered while \(\gcd(u_1,\dots,u_n)\)
does not stabilize above \(1\).

Lemma 1 (Finite cover implies finite compositeness):
If index set \(\{1,\dots,N\}\) is covered by congruence classes each forcing
\(u_n\equiv 0\pmod{q_j}\), then \(u_1,\dots,u_N\) are composite whenever
\(|u_n|>q_j\) on the covered classes.
\[
\textit{Proof.}
\]
For each \(n\le N\), choose a class containing \(n\). Then \(q_j\mid u_n\). If
\(|u_n|>q_j>1\), \(u_n\) is composite.
\(\square\)
Lemma 2 (Prefix gcd collapse criterion):
If two indices \(m,n\) satisfy \(\gcd(u_m,u_n)=1\), then
\[
\gcd(u_1,\dots,u_{\max\{m,n\}})=1.
\]
\[
\textit{Proof.}
\]
Any common divisor of the whole prefix divides both \(u_m\) and \(u_n\), hence is
\(1\).
\(\square\)
Lemma 3 (Reduction to infinite construction):
If one can build arbitrarily long prefixes satisfying Lemma 1 and with at least
one coprime pair from Lemma 2 in each prefix, then a diagonal construction gives
an infinite candidate with composite terms and no persistent gcd \(>1\).
\[
\textit{Proof sketch.}
\]
Choose an increasing sequence of prefixes with consistent initial seeds/covering
data; extract a convergent diagonal parameter choice preserving both properties at
every finite stage.
\(\square\)
