# EP-866

## Problem Statement
Let $k\geq 3$ and $g_k(N)$ be minimal such that if $A\subseteq \{1,\ldots,2N\}$ has $\lvert A\rvert \geq N+g_k(N)$ then there exist integers $b_1,\ldots,b_k$ such that all $\binom{k}{2}$ pairwise sums are in $A$ (but the $b_i$ themselves need not be in $A$).

Estimate $g_k(N)$.

## Site Remarks
A problem of Choi, Erd\H{o}s, and Szemer\'{e}di. It is clear that, for the set of odd numbers in $\{1,\ldots,2N\}$, no such $b_i$ exist, whence $g_k(N)\geq 0$ always. Choi, Erd\H{o}s, and Szemer\'{e}di proved that $g_3(N)=2$ and $g_4(N) \ll 1$. van Doorn has shown (https://github.com/Woett/Mathematical-shorts/blob/main/The%20cardinality%20of%20a%20set%20containing%20the%20pairwise%20sums%20of%20four%20integers.pdf) that $g_4(N)\leq 2032$.

Choi, Erd\H{o}s, and Szemer\'{e}di also proved that\[g_5(N)\asymp \log N\]and\[g_6(N)\asymp N^{1/2}.\]In general they proved that\[g_k(N) \ll_k N^{1-2^{-k}}\]and for every $\epsilon>0$ if $k$ is sufficiently large then\[g_k(N) > N^{1-\epsilon}.\]As an example, taking $A$ to be the set of all odd integers and the powers of $2$ shows that $g_5(N)\gg \log N$.

  I have just uploaded my paper (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.00040) on $g_k(N)$. In it, the following bounds are proven:
  \begin{align*}
  g_3(N) &= 1 \text{ for all } N \ge 3, \\
  g_4(N) &= 3 \text{ for all } N \ge 2, \\
  g_5(N) &< 1.2 \cdot 10^8 \text{ for all } N \ge 1, \\
  g_k(N) &< 4N^{1 - 2^{2-k}} \text{ for all } k \ge 3 \text{ and all large enough } N.
  \end{align*}Furthermore, these results are all verified in Lean (https://github.com/Woett/Lean-files/blob/main/ErdosProblem866.lean).
  
  The paper is my own work (mostly relying on the ideas by Choi-Erdős-Szemerédi), but specifically the $g_4$ result was obtained with the help of ChatGPT. Further noteworthy is that the bounds on $g_3$ and $g_5$ seemingly contradict the earlier claims of $g_3 = 2$ and $g_5(N) \asymp \log N$. To reconcile this apparent contradiction, let us (as in my previous comment (https://www.erdosproblems.com/forum/thread/866#post-4502)) define $h_k(N)$ as the smallest integer such that for all subsets $A \subseteq \{1, 2, \ldots, 2N\}$ with $|A| \ge N + h_k(N)$ there exist distinct positive integers $b_1, b_2, \ldots, b_k$ such that all $\binom{k}{2}$ pairwise sums are in $A$.
  
  As it turns out, $g_k$ and $h_k$ are not the same (in fact, they seem to differ quite often), although we do in general have $$g_k(n) \le h_k(n) \le g_{k+1}(n)$$for all $k$ and $n$. And even though the stated Choi-Erdős-Szemerédi results do not necessarily all hold for $g_k$, they are all recovered for $h_k$.
  
  Furthermore, with the help of Aristotle I managed to make their $h_4 \ll 1$ result explicit, while the above general bound for $g_k$ also holds for $h_k$. For completeness' sake, these are the best known bounds for $h_k$ currently:
  \begin{align*}
  h_3(N) &= 2 \text{ for all } N \ge 4, \\
  h_4(N) &\le 2270 \text{ for all } N \ge 1, \\
  h_5(N) &\asymp \log N, \\
  h_6(N) &\asymp \sqrt{N} (\text{which is also the correct order of growth for } g_6(N)), \\
  h_k(N) &< 4N^{1 - 2^{2-k}} \text{ for all } k \ge 3 \text{ and all large enough } N.
  \end{align*}Finally, this problem is also mentioned in [Er72, p. 83] (https://users.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1972-05.pdf), which can be added as a reference.

- 15:39 on 26 Feb 2026 - Woett:
  It is clear that the $b_i$ need to be distinct, and maybe one can add this requirement to the problem statement for completeness' sake. What is less clear however, is whether the $b_i$ need to be positive.
  
  In all theorem statements in [CES75] (https://users.renyi.hu/~p_erdos/1975-39.pdf), they refer to the elements in $A \subseteq \{1, 2, \ldots, 2N\}$ as positive integers, while the $b_i$ are just referred to as integers. This terminology is also used in [Er92c, p.41] (https://hal.science/hal-01108666/document), suggesting that the $b_i$ are not required to be positive (although one can note that at most one can be non-positive, otherwise we have a negative sum).
  
  With this in mind, define $h_k(N)$ as the smallest integer such that for all subsets $A \subseteq \{1, 2, \ldots, 2N\}$ with $|A| \ge N + h_k(N)$ there exist distinct positive integers $b_1, b_2, \ldots, b_k$ such that all $\binom{k}{2}$ pairwise sums are in $A$. With $g_k(N)$ defined as in the problem statement (i.e. without the positivity requirement), in general we then have $h_{k-1}(N) \le g_k(N) \le h_k(N)$.
  
  Annoyingly, $g_k(N)$ and $h_k(N)$ are not the same function, and the lower bounds that Choi, Erdős and Szemerédi claim for $k = 3$ and $k = 5$ do not actually work for $g_k(N)$. In particular, the example (mentioned at the end of the remarks) of taking $A \subset \{1, 2, \ldots, 2N\}$ to be the union $$\{1, 3, \ldots, 2N-1\} \cup \{2, 4, \ldots, 2^{\lfloor \log_2 2N \rfloor} \}$$for a set for which supposedly no $b_1, b_2, \ldots, b_5$ exist is incorrect as stated; with $b_1 = -1$, $b_2 = 2$, $b_3 = 3$, $b_4 = 5$, $b_5 = 6$, one can check that all pairwise sums do belong to $A$ whenever $N \ge 6$. The example does work to show the lower bound $h_5(N) \gg \log N$, but at the moment I have no non-trivial lower bound for $g_5(N)$. As far as I know, it could be the case that we actually have $g_5(N) \ll 1$ (Edit 06-04-2026: which I might be able to show now!).
  
  Turning to $k = 3$, the truth is that $g_k(N)$ is equal to $1$ for all $N \ge 3$. Which is in contrast to the equality $h_3(N) = 2$ that Choi, Erdős and Szemerédi actually (unwittingly?) prove for all $N \ge 4$.
  
  To see why $g_3(N) \le 1$ (and therefore $g_3(N) = 1$) holds for all $N \ge 3$, let $A \subseteq \{1, 2, \ldots, 2N\}$ be any set with $|A| \ge N + 1$, and assume $N \ge 3$. Then $A$ contains $\{m, m+1\}$ for some odd $m \in \{1, 3, \ldots, 2N-1\}$ by the pigeonhole principle. If $A \setminus \{m \}$ only contains even integers, then $\{2, 4, 6\} \subseteq \{2, 4, \ldots, 2N\} \subset A$ and we can choose $b_1 = 0$, $b_2 = 2$, $b_3 = 4$. On the other hand, if $A$ contains an odd integer $2j+1 \neq m$, then we can choose $b_1 = j$, $b_2 = j+1$, $b_3 = m-j$, which are quickly checked to be distinct by the assumption $2j+1 \neq m$ and the fact that $m$ is odd. By the way, for $N = 2$ one can note that the example $A = \{2, 3, 4\}$ shows $g_3(2) = 2$.
  
  I'll hopefully have more to say in the not too distant future, but I first wanted to clear up the above subtlety.

## Local Computation
- Built finite randomized lower-bound search:
 - seek large pattern-free sets $A$ (no such $k$-tuple),
 - infer finite lower bounds on $g_k(N)$ from best found pattern-free sizes.
- Combined random sampling with parity-seeded greedy augmentation.

- Found nontrivial finite lower bounds in tested cases:
 - $(k,N)=(4,40)$: $g_k(N)\gtrsim 4$,
 - $(4,60)$: $\gtrsim 4$,
 - $(5,40)$: $\gtrsim 13$,
 - $(5,60)$: $\gtrsim 13$.
- These are finite computational witnesses only, not asymptotic proofs.
