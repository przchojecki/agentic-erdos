# EP-726

## Problem Statement
As $n\to \infty$ ranges over integers\[\sum_{p\leq n}1_{n\in (p/2,p)\pmod{p}}\frac{1}{p}\sim \frac{\log\log n}{2}.\]

## Site Remarks
A conjecture of Erd\H{o}s, Graham, Ruzsa, and Straus \cite{EGRS75}. For comparison the classical estimate of Mertens states that\[\sum_{p\leq n}\frac{1}{p}\sim \log\log n.\]By $n\in (p/2,p)\pmod{p}$ we mean $n\equiv r\pmod{p}$ for some integer $r$ with $p/2<r<p$.

## Site References

  Most likely they were motivated by binomial coefficients, since the number of times $p$ divides $\binom{2n}{n}$ is equal to $1_{n \in (p/2,p) \hbox{ mod } p}$ plus some additional terms, by Kummer's theorem.

- 18:31 on 31 Aug 2025 - msawhney:
  A natural way to attempt to attack such a problem is to bound the exponential sum $\sum e(n/p)/p$. Via the method of bilinear sums, one is let to considering $\sum e(n/ab)$ and establishing cancellation when $a,b\sim \exp((\log n)^{o(1)})$. This appears to be out of reach of standard methods (in particular say Chapter 8 in Iwaniec--Kowalski). However by restricting to primes which are between $\exp((\log n)^{.99})$ and $n$ (say) one could plausibly prove a bound of $\gtrsim \log\log n$ and $(1-\Omega(1))\log\log n$.

- 19:14 on 31 Aug 2025 - TerenceTao (reply depth 1):
  A back of the envelope calculation suggests that Proposition 1.12 of this paper of Matomaki, Radziwill, Shao, Teravainen, and myself (https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.03335) should be able to handle the range $p \geq \exp(\log^{2/3+\varepsilon} n)$, which should give upper and lower bounds of $\gtrsim \frac{1}{6} \log \log n$ and $\lesssim \frac{5}{6} \log\log n$ if I did the calculations correctly.

- 00:02 on 04 Sep 2025 - msawhney (reply depth 2):
  Attacking this problem via such exponential sums does seem to require better handling issues which seem plausibly related to the zero free region of zeta. If one wants just an "average in n" version (e.g. such a stated holds for 100% of n) the problem does become substantially easier.

## Local Proof Attempts
The direct check was simply to evaluate the prime sum over a moderate grid of \(n\)-values and compare it against \((1/2)\log\log n\). The finite data follows the conjectured growth scale but stays below the predicted main term on this range, so it gives only soft support for the conjecture and no leverage on the asymptotic constant.
