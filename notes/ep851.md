# EP-851

## Problem Statement
Let $\epsilon>0$. Is there some $r\ll_\epsilon 1$ such that the density of integers of the form $2^k+n$, where $k\geq 0$ and $n$ has at most $r$ prime divisors, is at least $1-\epsilon$?

## Site Remarks
Romanoff \cite{Ro34} proved that the set of integers of the form $2^k+p$ (where $p$ is prime) has positive lower density.

This was solved (https://www.overleaf.com/read/svgbjzpxxppv#4eea7e), using GPT-5.2 Pro, by Price (see the comments). Sawhney in the comments claims that he and Green have a quantitative version of this argument that allows one to take\[r\ll \frac{\log (1/\epsilon)}{\log\log(1/\epsilon)}.\]See also [205].

- 19:29 on 05 Feb 2026 - TerenceTao (reply depth 1):
  This looks like a viable strategy to me. One can prove Romanoff's theorem by computing first and second moments of the representation function $r(n) = \sum_{2^k+p=n} 1$; sieve theory only gets an upper bound for the second moment that is off by a constant, so one only gets positive density in this fashion. The GPT proof works with the representation function $R(n) = \sum_{2^k+m=n} 1$ where $m$ is constrained to not be divisible by any prime between $z$ and $x^{1/t}$ for some large constants $z,t$ (which implies that $m$ has $O_{z,t}(1)$ prime divisors for $n \leq x$). Sieve theory is capable of estimating first and second moments here to high accuracy (if $t$ is large), and there are no irregularities coming from small primes (because $z$ is large), so this will in principle give the desired $1-\varepsilon$ type density just from the fundamental lemma of sieve theory. (The average number of solutions here is $\asymp t \log z$, so well above $1$ and so Chebyshev's inequality has a good shot at getting the density close to $1$ if one can also hold the variance to $O(t \log z)$ as one would expect from Poisson heuristics.)
  
  The proof should be streamlined and aligned with existing literature, preferably by a human expert, but this looks doable. Lean formalization might barely be possible with the existing sieve theory and other analytic number theory tools present in Mathlib and PNT+ but would be a non-trivial challenge. (I don't believe the fundamental lemma of sieve theory is in either repository yet, for instance.)
  
  EDIT: There is surprisingly little work on this problem, given that Romanoff's theorem is very well known (78 citations on MathSciNet). The closest recent reference I could find is this 2024 paper by Zhao (https://koreascience.or.kr/article/JAKO202405260120370.page), which focuses on counterexamples in an arithmetic progression (https://koreascience.or.kr/article/JAKO202405260120370.page) for any fixed $r$ using the covering congruence method, but this problem of Erdos seems to have been completely overlooked in the literature. AI Deep research tools were surprisingly useless here: Claude (https://claude.ai/share/d9c6af06-fe26-43ad-9ed4-b6ef056719a4) and ChatGPT Deep Research (https://chatgpt.com/share/6984f8b0-a16c-800e-94fa-655511d4c959) both confused this problem with #205. Gemini DeepResearch (https://gemini.google.com/share/4f2585d7f088) did give an accurate summary of related results, though lacking some of the most recent results I found with more traditional literature search.
  
  SECOND EDIT: I can now confirm that the proof is correct. After applying the fundamental lemma of sieve theory to estimate the first and second moments of $R$, the task boils down to showing that a singular series $\prod_{z \leq p \leq x^{1/t}: p | 2^k - 2^l} (1 + O(1/p))$ is close to $1$ on average after averaging over $k \neq l$. The condition $p | 2^k - 2^l$ can be rewritten as the condition that $k-l$ is a multiple of $\mathrm{ord}_2(p)$. Because of the very slow divergence of $\sum_p 1/p$, the only dangerous cases are those where $\mathrm{ord}_2(p)$ is of logarithmic size, but this occurs for very few primes $p$, because any Mersenne number $2^a-1$ can have at most $O(a)$ prime factors. (This is quantified in Lemma 7 of the AI-generated proof.)

(One can reduce this to a purely local problem however instead of a sieve theory task. The crucial thing is to allow various numbers under consideration to have an additional prime factor between $(\log n)$ and $(\log n)^{\omega(1)}$ say.
  
  In particular based on naive probabilistic considerations one might expect that $k(\epsilon) =2$; however covering congruences plus Bang's theorem (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zsigmondy%27s_theorem) explain this is false. This is explained in the work of Erdos and therefore proving that one can take $k(\epsilon)$ bounded requires at least Hough's celebrated result.
