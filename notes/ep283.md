# EP-283

## Problem Statement
Let $p:\mathbb{Z}\to \mathbb{Z}$ be a polynomial whose leading coefficient is positive and such that there exists no $d\geq 2$ with $d\mid p(n)$ for all $n\geq 1$. Is it true that, for all sufficiently large $m$, there exist integers $1\leq n_1<\cdots <n_k$ such that\[1=\frac{1}{n_1}+\cdots+\frac{1}{n_k}\]and\[m=p(n_1)+\cdots+p(n_k)?\]

## Site Remarks
Graham \cite{Gr63} has proved this when $p(x)=x$. Graham also conjectures that this remains true with $1$ replaced by an arbitrary rational $\alpha>0$ (provided $m$ is taken sufficiently large depending on $\alpha$).

Cassels \cite{Ca60} has proved that these conditions on the polynomial imply every sufficiently large integer is the sum of $p(n_i)$ with distinct $n_i$. Burr has proved this if $p(x)=x^k$ with $k\geq 1$ and if we allow $n_i=n_j$.

Alekseyev \cite{Al19} has proved this when $p(x)=x^2$, for all $m>8542$. For example,\[1=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{6}+\frac{1}{12}\]and\[200 = 2^2+4^2+6^2+12^2.\]van Doorn \cite{vD25} has investigated the question of what 'sufficiently large' means for $p(x)=x$. van Doorn has also proved the original conjecture for many linear and quadratic polynomials, for example $p(x)=x+5$ or $p(x)=x^2+100$ - see the comments section.

- 00:13 on 28 Mar 2026 - Woett:
  Specifying to the polynomial $p(x) = x$, in [Gr63] (https://mathweb.ucsd.edu/~ronspubs/63_02_partitions.pdf) Graham more generally proved that for all positive rationals $\alpha$ and all $m \in \mathbb{N}$, there exists an $n_{\alpha, m}$ such that for all positive integers $n \ge n_{\alpha, m}$ there exist distinct integers $a_1, \ldots, a_r$, all larger than or equal to $m$, with $$n = a_1 + \cdots + a_r \hspace{15pt} \text{and} \hspace{15pt} \alpha = \frac{1}{a_1} + \cdots + \frac{1}{a_r}.$$His proof was non-constructive however, and he wrote that determining the value of $n_{\alpha, m}$ 'seems to be a difficult question'.
  
  It is clear that one needs at least almost all integers in $[m, e^{\alpha}m]$ before the reciprocal sum even reaches $\alpha$, so one can check that a quick lower bound on $n_{\alpha, m}$ is the sum of all integers in $[m, (1-o(1))e^{\alpha}m]$, which gives $$n_{\alpha, m} \ge \left(\frac{1}{2} - o(1)\right)(e^{2\alpha} - 1)m^2.$$On the other hand, by Croot's solution to #284 (https://www.erdosproblems.com/284) and #286 (https://www.erdosproblems.com/286) (which should link to one another by the way), it is actually possible to write $\alpha$ as a sum of reciprocals whose denominators are, for large $m$, all contained in $[m, (1+o_{\alpha}(1))e^{\alpha}m]$. So we deduce that there is at least one $n = \left(\frac{1}{2} + o_{\alpha}(1)\right)(e^{2\alpha} - 1)m^2$ for which such $a_i$ exist. Croot's result does not give finer control over the sum of the denominators however, so it is a priori unclear whether this can be leveraged to prove that for all $n \ge \left(\frac{1}{2} + o_{\alpha}(1)\right)(e^{2\alpha} - 1)m^2$ such $a_i$ exist.
  
  Fortunately, it turns out to be possible to combine the ideas and results of Graham and Croot, in order to show that (for fixed $\alpha$ and large $m$) we have the asymptotic $$n_{\alpha, m} = \left(\frac{1}{2} - o_{\alpha}(1)\right)(e^{2\alpha} - 1)m^2.$$A proof of this asymptotic can be found in this (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.02200) paper of mine from last year, which I also linked to in an earlier comment.
  
  The proof naturally relies on Croot's results (Proposition $1$ and $2$ from this (https://arxiv.org/pdf/math/9904181) paper, to be precise), as well as on the fact that smooth integers have positive density in every residue class. I felt that, conditional on these results, the rest of the proof was within range of autoformalization. And Aristotle succeeded (https://github.com/Woett/Lean-files/blob/main/ExplicitGraham.lean)! Requiring almost $4000$ lines of code and a decent amount of computation, with some patience one can type-check it online here (https://live.lean-lang.org/#project=mathlib-v4.28.0&url=https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Woett/Lean-files/refs/heads/main/ExplicitGraham.lean).
  
  I know the above is slightly off-topic as it doesn't directly deal with the more general problem at hand, but I thought it was still sufficiently interesting and thereby (hopefully) worth sharing.

- 16:01 on 11 Aug 2025 - Woett:
  In the $1963$ paper by Graham that is mentioned, he more generally asks whether for every such polynomial $p(x)$ and every positive rational $\alpha$ there exists an $n_0 = n_0(p(x), \alpha)$ such that for all $n \ge n_0$ there exist distinct positive integers $a_1, \ldots, a_r$ such that $\alpha = \frac{1}{a_1} + \cdots + \frac{1}{a_r}$ and $n = p(a_1) + \cdots + p(a_r)$.
  
  Graham proves that, if we consider the polynomial $p(x) = x$, then such an $n_0$ indeed exists for all $\alpha > 0$. He was not able to give any bound on $n_0$, however.
  
  At the risk of self-promotion, I recently put a paper on the arXiv (see here (https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.02200)) that deals with precisely this question. In particular I prove that for all $\epsilon > 0$ there exists a constant $c$ such that for all positive rationals $\alpha = \frac{p}{q}$ we have the upper bound $$n_0(x, \alpha) < \frac{cq \log^3 q}{\min(\alpha, 1) \log \log q} + c(e + \epsilon)^{2 \alpha}.$$Furthermore, this upper bound is in many cases fairly optimal. Indeed, the $c(e + \epsilon)^{2 \alpha}$ term is (up to the $\epsilon$) required, as one can check that we always have $n_0(x, \alpha) \gg e^{2 \alpha}$. And as for the first part of the upper bound, in the linked paper I also show that we infinitely often have $n_0(x, \alpha) > 0.3 q \log^2 q$.

## Local Computation
Finite compute signal:
 - Random split-generated decompositions of 1 produced many attainable m=\sum p(n_i) values.
 - Coverage is nontrivial for p(x)=x and much sparser for p(x)=x^2 in tested finite ranges.

 - Supports abundance heuristics for linear-type polynomials; quadratic and general-polynomial asymptotics are still hard.

- Interpretation: finite experiment extends local evidence but does not close the asymptotic conjectural gap; next step is parameter scaling or a structural lemma matched to this EP.

## Local Proof Attempts
Target:
For polynomial \(p(x)\), characterize integers
\[
m=\sum_i p(n_i)
\]
arising from Egyptian-fraction decompositions of \(1\), and prove eventual
coverage/noncoverage laws by polynomial class.
Step A: Decomposition algebra
Translate split operations on unit fractions into additive moves on \(m\)-values.
Step B: Semigroup generation analysis
Show reachable \(m\)-set forms an additive semigroup (or near-semigroup with finite
defect) generated by polynomial increment templates.
Step C: Asymptotic density/classification
Use semigroup structure to deduce abundance for linear-type \(p\) and sparse
behavior criteria for higher-degree \(p\).
Concrete Blocking Lemma (Most Critical):
Need a closure lemma proving that standard Egyptian split moves induce a finite
generating set of \(m\)-increments for a given polynomial class.

Lemma 1 (Split identity):
For \(a,b>0\),
\[
\frac1a=\frac1{a+b}+\frac1{a(a+b)/b}
\]
whenever \(b\mid a(a+b)\). This creates a legal refinement of an Egyptian
decomposition.
\[
\textit{Proof.}
\]
Compute RHS:
\[
\frac{1}{a+b}+\frac{b}{a(a+b)}=\frac{a+b}{a(a+b)}=\frac1a.
\]
\(\square\)
Lemma 2 (Additive update on polynomial weight):
If a decomposition step replaces denominator \(a\) by \(u,v\), then weighted sum
updates by
\[
m\mapsto m-p(a)+p(u)+p(v).
\]
\[
\textit{Proof.}
\]
Definition of \(m=\sum p(n_i)\) is additive over terms.
\(\square\)
Lemma 3 (Semigroup reduction):
If the set of possible increments
\[
I:=\{-p(a)+p(u)+p(v)\}
\]
from admissible splits is finite modulo gcd \(g\), then reachable \(m\)-values are
eventually periodic modulo \(g\) and form finite-union translates of a numerical
semigroup.
\[
\textit{Proof sketch.}
\]
Repeated application of Lemma 2 yields walk on \(\mathbb Z\) by increments in
\(I\). Standard numerical-semigroup/automaton arguments give eventual periodicity
modulo \(g=\gcd(I)\).
\(\square\)
