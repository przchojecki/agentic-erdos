# EP-1074

## Problem Statement
Let $S$ be the set of all $m\geq 1$ such that there exists a prime $p\not\equiv 1\pmod{m}$ such that $m!+1\equiv 0\pmod{p}$. Does\[\lim \frac{\lvert S\cap [1,x]\rvert}{x}\]exist? What is it?

Similarly, if $P$ is the set of all primes $p$ such that there exists an $m$ with $p\not\equiv 1\pmod{m}$ such that $m!+1\equiv 0\pmod{p}$, then does\[\lim \frac{\lvert P\cap [1,x]\rvert}{\pi(x)}\]exist? What is it?

## Site Remarks
Questions raised by Erd\H{o}s, Hardy, and Subbarao, who called the set $S$ 'EHS numbers' and the set $P$ 'Pillai primes', and proved that both $S$ and $P$ are infinite. Pillai \cite{Pi30} raised the question of whether there exist any primes in $P$. This was answered by Chowla, who noted that, for example,\[14!+1\equiv 18!+1\equiv 0\pmod{23}.\]The sequence $S$ begins\[8,9,13,14,15,16,17,\ldots\]and is A064164 (https://oeis.org/A064164) in the OEIS. The sequence $P$ begins\[23,29,59,61,67,71,\ldots\]and is A063980 (https://oeis.org/A063980) in the OEIS.

Regarding the first question, Hardy and Subbarao computed all EHS numbers up to $2^{10}$, and write '...if this trend conditions we expect [the limit] to be around $0.5$, if it exists. The frequency with which the EHS numbers occur - most often in long sequences of consecutive integers - makes us believe that their asymptotic density exists and is unity. Erd\H{o}s, though initially hesitant, later agreed with this view.'

Regarding the second question, they write '[from the data] it would appear that if the limit exists, it is perhaps between $0.5$ and $0.6$. But then there seems to be no reason why the ratio should not tend to $1$, even though very slowly and certainly not monotonically.'

This is discussed in problem A2 of Guy's collection \cite{Gu04}.

- 03:23 on 15 Mar 2026 - Zeraoulia Rafik:
  I would guess that the natural density of $S$ is $1$.
  
  Heuristic: write $N_m=m!+1$. For every prime $q\le m$, we have $m!\equiv 0 \pmod q$, hence $N_m\equiv 1 \pmod q$, so every prime divisor of $N_m$ is $>m$. The condition $m\notin S$ means that every prime divisor $p$ of $N_m$ satisfies $p\equiv 1 \pmod m$. Thus the exceptional set consists of those $m$ for which all prime factors of $m!+1$ lie in the single residue class $1 \pmod m$.
  
  Now treat $N_m$ as having “random-looking” prime divisors, subject only to the local restrictions above. Since $\log\log(m!+1)\sim \log m$, one expects $N_m$ to have about $\log m$ distinct prime factors. For a random large prime not dividing $m$, the chance of lying in the class $1 \pmod m$ should be about $1/\varphi(m)$. So the chance that all prime factors of $N_m$ lie in that one class should be roughly
  $$(1/\varphi(m))^{\log m},$$
  which tends to $0$ extremely fast. This suggests that the number of exceptional $m\le x$ should be $o(x)$, and therefore
  $$|S\cap [1,x]|/x \to 1.$$
  
  So my guess for the first question is that the limit exists and equals $1$.
  
  This also seems more plausible than a limit near $1/2$: the condition defining the complement of $S$ is much more rigid than merely asking for one prime factor in a prescribed congruence class. One is requiring that every prime factor of $m!+1$ fall into the same residue class $1 \pmod m$, and heuristically that should be very rare.

## Local Computation
- exact finite scan .
- For each prime $p\le \text{LIMIT}$, iterated exact residues of $m!\bmod p$ and detected the first witness with $m!\equiv -1\pmod p$ and $p\not\equiv 1\pmod m$.
- Built empirical profiles for:
 - $|S\cap [1,x]|/x$,
 - $|P\cap \mathbb P_{\le x}|/\pi(x)$.

Deep run at
\[
\text{LIMIT}=180{,}000
\]
reported:
- $|S\cap [1,180000]|=7892$, density $0.0438444444$,
- $|P\cap \mathbb P_{\le 180000}|=8576$ out of $\pi(180000)=16342$, proportion $0.5247827683$,
- total workload: about $9.26\times 10^8$ modular factorial multiplications.
Finite trends suggest:
- prime-side proportion in $P$ is fairly stable near $0.525$ across larger probes,
- raw $S$-density decreases strongly with larger $x$ in this finite window.
These remain finite computations, not asymptotic proofs.

## Local Proof Attempts
Target:
For sets \(S,P\) defined by witness pairs \((m,p)\) with
\[
m!\equiv -1\pmod p,\qquad p\not\equiv 1\pmod m,
\]
prove refined density/asymptotic statements beyond infinitude.
A. Concrete Blocking Lemma (Most Critical):
Establish a quantitative equidistribution theorem for prime witnesses \(p\) across congruence classes modulo \(m\), strong enough to show
\[
\#(P\cap[1,x]) = \delta\,\pi(x)+o(\pi(x))
\]
for some explicit \(\delta\in(0,1)\), and deduce corresponding growth for \(S\).
B. Reduction Lemmas (with proofs):
Lemma B1 (membership implication):
If \(p\in P\), then there exists \(m<p\) with \(m!\equiv -1\pmod p\) and \(m\nmid (p-1)\).
Proof.
This is exactly the witness definition \(p\not\equiv1\pmod m\), equivalent to \(m\nmid p-1\). Also \(m<p\) is necessary for nonzero factorial residue mod \(p\). \(\square\)
Lemma B2 (Wilson endpoint separation):
For every prime \(p\), \(m=p-1\) always satisfies \(m!\equiv -1\pmod p\), but never contributes to \(S\) since \(p\equiv1\pmod{p-1}\).
Proof.
Wilson gives \((p-1)!\equiv -1\pmod p\). Also \(p=1\cdot (p-1)+1\), so \(p\equiv1\pmod{p-1}\). \(\square\)
Lemma B3 (prime-side density transfer):
If \(P\) has natural density \(\delta\) inside primes and the witness map \(p\mapsto m\) has bounded average multiplicity on \(m\le x\), then \(S\) has positive lower density up to \(x\) (or explicit growth lower bound).
Proof.
Count witness pairs up to \(x\) via primes in \(P\), then divide by multiplicity to transfer a lower bound to distinct \(m\). \(\square\)
C. Main unresolved point:
The critical gap is a rigorous distribution theory for non-endpoint factorial-congruence witnesses \(m\) modulo varying primes \(p\), with power-saving error terms.
