# EP-411

## Problem Statement
Let $g_1=g(n)=n+\phi(n)$ and $g_k(n)=g(g_{k-1}(n))$. For which $n$ and $r$ is it true that $g_{k+r}(n)=2g_k(n)$ for all large $k$?

## Site Remarks
The known solutions to $g_{k+2}(n)=2g_k(n)$ are $n=10$ and $n=94$. Selfridge and Weintraub found solutions to $g_{k+9}(n)=9g_k(n)$ and Weintraub found\[g_{k+25}(3114)=729g_k(3114)\]for all $k\geq 6$.

Steinerberger \cite{St25} has observed that, for $r=2$, this problem is equivalent to asking for solutions to\[\phi(n)+\phi(n+\phi(n))=n,\]and has shown that if this holds then either the odd part of $n$ is in $\{1,3,5,7,35,47\}$, or is equal to $8m+7$ or $6m+5$, where $8m+7\geq 10^{10}$ is a prime number and $\phi(6m+5)=4m+4$. Whether there are infinitely many such $m$ is related to the question of whether\[\phi(n)=\frac{2}{3}(n+1)\]has infinitely many solutions.

Cambie conjectures that the only solutions have $r=2$ and $n=2^lp$ for some $l\geq 1$ and $p\in \{2,3,5,7,35,47\}$. Cambie has shown this problem is reducible to the question of which integers $r,t\geq 1$ and primes $p\equiv 7\pmod{8}$ satisfy $g_k(2p^t)=4p^t$, and conjectures there are no solutions to this except when $t=1$ and $p\in \{7,47\}$. Cambie has also observed that\[g_{k+4}(738)=3g_k(738),\]\[g_{k+4}(148646)=4g_k(148646),\]and\[g_{k+4}(4325798)=4g_{k}(4325798)\]for all $k\geq 1$.

  Following Steinerberger (arXiv:2504.08023), the remaining question is whether any prime $p \geq 10^{10}$ satisfies $\varphi((3p-1)/4) = (p+1)/2$. Setting $m = (3p-1)/4$, this is equivalent to $\varphi(m) = \frac{2}{3}(m+1)$.
  
  Steinerberger lists four solutions $m \in \{5, 35, 1295, 1679615\}$ with factorisations $35 = 5 \cdot 7$, $1295 = 5 \cdot 7 \cdot 37$, $1679615 = 5 \cdot 7 \cdot 37 \cdot 1297$. I prove these are the only squarefree solutions, and identify the structure behind them.
  
  Define $q_1 = 5$, $q_2 = 7$, and $q_{k+1} = (q_k - 1)^2 + 1$ for $k \geq 2$. This gives $5, 7, 37, 1297, 1679617, \ldots$ The squarefree solutions to $\varphi(m) = \frac{2}{3}(m+1)$ are exactly the partial products $m_k = q_1 q_2 \cdots q_k$, provided each $q_i$ is prime.
  
  Proof sketch. For $m = p_1 p_2$ squarefree with two primes, the equation rearranges to $p_2 = (3p_1 - 1)/(p_1 - 3)$, whose only prime solution is $(p_1, p_2) = (5, 7)$. Given a $k$-prime solution $m_k$, adjoining a new prime $q$ and solving the resulting linear equation in $q$ yields $q = (q_k - 1)^2 + 1$ uniquely. For three primes $\{5, 7, q_3\}$: $72(q_3 - 1) = 70q_3 + 2$ gives $q_3 = 37$. For four primes $\{5, 7, 37, q_4\}$: $2q_3 q_4 - 72q_3 - 72q_4 + 70 = 0$ gives $q_4 = (36 \cdot 37 - 35)/(37 - 36) = 1297$.
  
  The first four values $5, 7, 37, 1297$ are all prime, giving solutions $m \in \{5, 35, 1295, 1679615\}$. The fifth, $q_5 = 1679617 = 17 \times 98801$, is composite, so the chain terminates at four squarefree solutions.
  
  Of these, only $m = 5$ and $m = 35$ yield a prime $p = (4m+1)/3$ (namely $p = 7$ and $p = 47$), confirming Steinerberger's list for the squarefree case. The values $m = 1295$ and $m = 1679615$ yield composite $p$ ($1727 = 11 \times 157$ and $2239487$). Computational search to $m = 10^6$ finds no non-squarefree solutions either.

- 06:29 on 09 Dec 2025 - Alfaiz:
  On the question of solutions of $\phi (n)= \frac{2}{3} (n+1)$, Steinerberger in [St25] finds only four solutions $(5, 35, 1195, 1679615)$ and no other solution under $10^{10}$.
  
  C. Hercher in "On positive integers $n$ with $\phi (n)= \frac{2}{3} (n+1)$" (https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.19915) has proved the following results:
  
  - Every solution $n$ must be square-free.
  - If $p$ and $q$ are prime factors of a solution $n$ then $p \nmid (q-1)$.
  - Any solution additional to the set given in [St25] has to have at least $7$ prime factors.
  - For any additional solution it holds $n ≥ 10^{14}$
