# EP-176

## Problem Statement
Let $N(k,\ell)$ be the minimal $N$ such that for any $f:\{1,\ldots,N\}\to\{-1,1\}$ there must exist a $k$-term arithmetic progression $P$ such that\[ \left\lvert \sum_{n\in P}f(n)\right\rvert\geq \ell.\]Find good upper bounds for $N(k,\ell)$. Is it true that for any $c>0$ there exists some $C>1$ such that\[N(k,ck)\leq C^k?\]What about\[N(k,2)\leq C^k\]or\[N(k,\sqrt{k})\leq C^k?\]

## Site Remarks
When $\ell=k$ this is the van der Waerden number $W(k)$ (see [138]). Spencer \cite{Sp73} has proved that if $k=2^tm$ with $m$ odd then\[N(k,1)=2^t(k-1)+1.\]Erd\H{o}s and Graham write that 'no decent bound' is known even for $N(k,2)$.

Erd\H{o}s \cite{Er63d} proved that, for every $c>0$,\[N(k,ck)> (1+\alpha_c)^k\]where $\alpha_c\to 0$ as $c\to 0$ and $\alpha_c\to \sqrt{2}-1$ as $c\to 1$.

Hunter in the comment section observes that the local lemma implies an improved bound of\[N(k,ck) \gg \frac{2^k}{k^{O(1)}\sum_{i>\frac{1+c}{2}k}\binom{k}{i}},\]so that in particular as $c\to 1$ we have, for all large $k$, $N(k,ck)\geq (2-o(1))^k$ (where the $o(1)$ term $\to 0$ as $c\to 1$).

Step A: Discrepancy lower constructions
Build \(\{-1,1\}\)-colorings on long intervals keeping all \(k\)-AP sums below
\(\ell\).
Step B: Fourier/entropy upper forcing
Show sufficiently long intervals force a \(k\)-AP with discrepancy at least
\(\ell\).
Step C: Asymptotic matching
Align lower and upper growth rates.
Concrete Blocking Lemma (Most Critical):
Need a sharp large-deviation estimate for AP-sum distributions under optimized
colorings, uniform in \(k\).

Proposition BQ1 (Monotonicity in \(N\)):
For fixed \(k,\ell\), if every coloring of \([1,N]\) has a \(k\)-AP with
\(|\sum_P f|\ge \ell\), then the same holds for all \(N'>N\). Hence \(N(k,\ell)\)
is well-defined once existence is known.
\[
\textit{Proof.}
\]
Restrict any coloring on \([1,N']\) to \([1,N]\).
\(\square\)
Proposition BQ2 (Exponential-scale reduction):
If one proves constants \(a_\ell,b_\ell>1\) with
\[
a_\ell^k \le N(k,\ell)\le b_\ell^k
\]
for large \(k\), then the growth is exponential in \(k\).
\[
\textit{Proof.}
\]
Take logarithms and divide by \(k\).
\(\square\)
