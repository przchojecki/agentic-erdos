# EP-468

## Problem Statement
For any $n$ let $D_n$ be the set of sums of the shape $d_1,d_1+d_2,d_1+d_2+d_3,\ldots$ where $1<d_1<d_2<\cdots$ are the divisors of $n$.

What is the size of $D_n\backslash \cup_{m<n}D_m$?

If $f(N)$ is the minimal $n$ such that $N\in D_n$ then is it true that $f(N)=o(N)$? Perhaps just for almost all $N$?

- 22:50 on 01 Nov 2025 - TerenceTao:
  I think one can disprove that $f(N) = o(N)$ for all $N$ as follows. Let $\sigma_k(n)$ denote the sum of the divisors of $n$ excluding the top $k$ factors (or $0$, if the number of divisors $\tau(n)$ is at most $k$). It is a standard calculation (factoring $n=dm$) that
  $$ \sum_{n \leq x} \sigma(n) = \sum_{d \leq x} d \sum_{m \leq x/d} 1 \ll \sum_{d \leq x} x \ll x^2,$$
  so $\sigma(n) = O(x)$ on the average. Suppose that we can more generally show
  $$ \sum_{n \leq x} \sigma_k(n) \ll x^2 / k^{1.1} \quad (1)$$
  for all $k \geq 1$. By Markov's inequality and the union bound, and the summability of $\sum_k 1/k^{1.1}$, we conclude that for any $C_0$, the number of $n \leq x$ such that $\sigma_k(n) \geq C_0 x$ for at least one $k$ is at most $O( x / C_0 )$, hence we have $f(N) \leq x$ for at most $O(x/C_0)$ of the $N \in [C_0 x, 2C_0 x]$, which is enough to disprove $f(N) = o(N)$ by taking $C_0$ large enough.
  
  It remains to prove (1). In order for a factor $d$ of $n=dm$ to contribute to $\sigma_k(n)$, there must be at least $k$ factors of $dm$ that are at most $m$, so either $\tau(m) \gg k^{0.1}$ or $d$ has $\gg k^{0.9}$ factors of size at most $m$. The contribution of the first case is at most
  $$ \sum_{m \leq x} (\tau(m)/k^{0.1})^{100} \sum_{d \leq x/m} d \ll \frac{x^2}{k^{10}} \sum_m \frac{\tau(m)^{100}}{m^2} \ll \frac{x^2}{k^{10}}$$
  which is acceptable (one could also have used the divisor bound $\tau(m) = m^{o(1)}$ here). The contribution of the second case is at most
  $$ \sum_{m \leq x} \sum_{d \leq x/m} d (\sum_{r \leq m: r|d} 1 / k^{0.9})^2$$
  $$ \ll \sum_{m } \sum_{r_1,r_2 \leq m} \frac{x^2}{m^2 [r_1,r_2] k^{1.8}}$$
  $$ \ll \sum_{m } \sum_{r_1,r_2} \frac{x^2}{m^{1.8} [r_1,r_2]^{1.1} k^{1.8}}$$
  $$ \ll \frac{x^2}{k^{1.8}}$$
  which is also acceptable (there is a lot of room to spare here ultimately coming from the fact that divisor functions are typically logarithmic in size, so presumably one could clean up this argument somehow; in particular, standard anatomy of integers heuristics predicts that (1) should in fact exhibit exponential decay in $k$).

- 17:03 on 02 Nov 2025 - TerenceTao (reply depth 4):
  We need to sum in $k$, which is in principle unbounded (in particular, it can be large compared to $C_0$), because the definition of $f(N)$ does not specify $k$ in advance.
  
  More precisely, one has $f(N) = \inf_k f_k(N)$, where $f_k(N)$ is the least $n$ for which $\sigma_k(n)=N$ (or $\infty$ if no such $n$ exists). The "trivial" bound controls each individual event $f_k(N) \leq x$, but the event $f(N) \leq x$ is the union of all such events and so one needs to sum in $k$ if one wishes to use the union bound.

- 22:24 on 14 Sep 2025 - Vjeko_Kovac:
  I thought I'd share some experimental data on this problem. I don't have any other ambitions with it. In fact, these sums of divisors behave so erratically that I'll be amazed if someone can prove anything nontrivial rigorously and unconditionally.
  
  I entered the sizes of sets $D'_n:=D_n\setminus\cup_{m<n}D_m$ as OEIS sequence A387502 (https://oeis.org/A387502). It is unclear what Erdős and Graham wanted to know about these numbers. There are many zeros in the sequence, i.e., many empty sets $D'_n$ (also see below), which can be conditionally deduced from (certain special cases of Schinzel's hypothesis), say, having infinitely primes $r$ of the form $r=pq+p+q$, $p<q$ primes, since then $D'_r=\emptyset$. On the other hand, the sets $D'_n$ can likely become arbitrarily large: the largest one I could find is $D'_{75600}$ of cardinality $24$. The very likely candidates are the numbers $n$ with large $\sigma(n)$. In reality, the record values of the cardinalities of $D'_n$ are attained for $n=2, 12, 36, 144, 336, 1320, 1980, 5040, 8400, 25200, 75600$.
  
  I also entered sizes of the unions $\cup_{m\leq n}D_m=\cup_{m\leq n}D'_m$ as OEIS sequence A387503 (https://oeis.org/A387503). From it one can see that the first $1000$ sets $D'_n$ have average size $0.924$, the first $10000$ have average size $0.8697$, and I also computed that the first $100000$ sets $D'_n$ have average size $0.8349$. This should also hint again that a positive share of the sets $D'_n$ are empty.
  
  Regarding the behavior of the function $f$, the same problem is also mentioned in the book by Guy (https://link.springer.com/book/10.1007/978-0-387-26677-0) and attributed to Erdős. There is a slight difference in the book: that the number $1$ is always counted in as the smallest divisor, but effectively this makes no difference, and the function $f$ only gets shifted by $1$. It seems that Guy's book was a better-known reference, since the values of $f$ already appear as OEIS sequence A167485 (https://oeis.org/A167485) (but again, note the indices shifted by $1$). Function $f$ is clearly undefined for $n=1,4$ (as the two smallest divisors $>1$ cannot add up to $1$ or $4$), and I'm not sure if it is known to be well-defined for all other $n$ (but experimentally it seems so). OEIS mentions this following from a certain stronger version of Goldbach's conjecture, but it would also follow from versions of Vingradov's theorem. For instance if sufficiently (but concretely) large odd $N$ are sums of distinct primes $p,q,r\in[x,x^2]$, then $N$ appears in the set $D_{pqr}$, which makes $f(N)$ finite (i.e., well-defined).
  
  The claim $f(N)=o(N)$ seems experimentally unlikely. In fact, $31.279\%$ of the numbers $N\leq100000$ even have $f(N)>100000$. Similarly, only less than $36\%$ of the ratios $f(N)/N$, for $5\leq N\leq10000$, are $\leq1$, only less than $27\%$ of them are $\leq0.9$, and only less than $6\%$ of them are $\leq0.5$: quite unlikely if one had $f(N)/N\to0$. A very easy observation (mentioned in the OEIS comments) is that $\limsup_{n\to\infty}\sigma(n)/n=\infty$ by taking $N=\sigma(n)-1$ implies $\liminf_{N\to\infty}f(N)/N=0$, so at least this is an unconditional but rather trivial result.

Prove asymptotic representability profile for \(D_n\) prefix-divisor sums and inverse function
\[
f(N)=\min\{n:\ N\in D_n\},
\]
in particular \(f(N)=o(N)\).
A. Concrete Blocking Lemma (Most Critical):
Show a high-density covering lemma: union \(\bigcup_{n\le X} D_n\) covers all but \(o(X)\) integers up to \(X\), with quantitative novelty bound per \(n\).
B. Reduction Lemmas (with proofs):
Lemma B1 (inverse equivalence):
\[
f(N)\le X \iff N\in \bigcup_{n\le X} D_n.
\]
Proof.
By definition of minimal witnessing index. \(\square\)
Lemma B2 (coverage-to-growth transfer):
If uncovered count up to \(X\) is \(o(X)\), then for density-1 set of \(N\), \(f(N)\le X\) with \(X=(1+o(1))N\), and sharper coverage gives \(f(N)=o(N)\).
Proof.
Apply B1 and monotonicity with chosen \(X\)-scales; sparse uncovered set forces early representation for almost all \(N\). \(\square\)
Lemma B3 (A implies \(f(N)=o(N)\)):
If A gives coverage at scales \(X=\eta N\) for every fixed \(\eta>0\) beyond thresholds, then \(f(N)\le \eta N\) eventually for represented \(N\), hence \(f(N)=o(N)\).
Proof.
Let \(\eta\downarrow0\) along sequence and use B1. \(\square\)

The bottleneck is proving strong enough cumulative coverage from sparse per-\(n\) novelty increments.
