# EP-1093

## Problem Statement
For $n\geq 2k$ we define the deficiency of $\binom{n}{k}$ as follows. If $\binom{n}{k}$ is divisible by a prime $p\leq k$ then the deficiency is undefined. Otherwise, the deficiency is the number of $0\leq i<k$ such that $n-i$ is $k$-smooth, that is, divisible only by primes $\leq k$.

Are there infinitely many binomial coefficients with deficiency $1$? Are there only finitely many with deficiency $>1$?

## Site Remarks
A problem of Erd\H{o}s, Lacampagne, and Selfridge \cite{ELS88}, that was also asked in the 1986 problem session of West Coast Number Theory (as reported here (https://westcoastnumbertheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/02/wcnt-problems-1991.pdf)).

In \cite{ELS93} they prove that if the deficiency exists and is $\geq 1$ then $n\ll 2^k\sqrt{k}$.

The following examples are either from \cite{ELS88} or here (https://westcoastnumbertheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/02/wcnt-problems-1991.pdf). The following have deficiency $1$ (there are $58$ examples with $n\leq 10^5$):\[\binom{7}{3},\binom{13}{4},\binom{14}{4},\binom{23}{5},\binom{62}{6},\binom{94}{10},\binom{95}{10}.\]The examples which follow are the only known examples with deficiency $>1$. The following have deficiency $2$:\[\binom{44}{8},\binom{74}{10},\binom{174}{12},\binom{239}{14},\binom{5179}{27},\binom{8413}{28},\binom{8414}{28},\binom{96622}{42}.\]The following have deficiency $3$:\[\binom{46}{10},\binom{47}{10},\binom{241}{16},\binom{2105}{25},\binom{1119}{27},\binom{6459}{33}.\]The following has deficiency $4$:\[\binom{47}{11}.\]The following has deficiency $9$:\[\binom{284}{28}.\]See also [384] and [1094].

Barreto in the comments has given a positive answer to the second question, conditional on two (strong) conjectures.

  For $\binom{n}{k}$ with defined deficiency (i.e. $\gcd\left(\binom{n}{k},\,k!\right)=1$), we shall write $\delta(n,k)$ for its associated deficiency. This is probably useless, but I can give a resolution to the second problem under two rather strong conjectures:
  
  $\textbf{Conjecture A}$ (Strengthening of the $xyz_{\mathrm{fin}}$-conjecture (https://arxiv.org/pdf/0911.4147) of Lagarias-Soundararajan). There exists $\kappa_0>1$ such that for all $\varepsilon>0$ there are only finitely many primitive integer triples $X+Y=Z$ with $$\max\{p\in\mathbb{P}:p\mid XYZ\}=:S(X,Y,Z)<(\log H(X,Y,Z))^{\kappa_0-\varepsilon},$$
  where $H(X,Y,Z):=\max\{|X|,\,|Y|,\,|Z|\}$.
  
  $\textbf{Conjecture B}$ (Height forcing). Let $h_2(k)$ be the least $n\geq 2k$ such that $\binom{n}{k}$ has defined deficiency $\delta(n,k)\geq 2$. There exists $\alpha>1/\kappa_0$ such that for all sufficiently large $k$, $$h_2(k)<\infty\implies\log h_2(k)\geq ck^{\alpha}$$for some fixed $c>0$. (By PNT, we can at least show that if there is such an $\alpha>0$, then $\alpha\leq 1$).
  
  Now, we state our result:
  
  $\textbf{Theorem}$. Assume Conjecture A and Conjecture B. Then, there are only finitely many pairs $(n,k)$ with $n\geq 2k$ and $\delta(n,k)\geq 2$.
  $\textit{Proof.}$ Suppose, for contradiction, that there are infinitely many such $(n,k)$. Fix one with $\delta(n,k)\geq 2$, then $\exists i,j\in\mathbb{N}$ with $i\neq j$ such that $x:= n-i$ and $y:=n-j$ are both $k$-smooth, lying in $(n-k,n]$. Set $\Delta:=\left|x-y\right|=\left|i-j\right|$, so $1\leq\Delta<k$. Since $\Delta<k$, all prime factors of $\Delta$ are $\leq k$, hence $\Delta$ is $k$-smooth. Now, consider the equation $y+\Delta=x$. Let $d:=\gcd(y,\Delta)$, and set $$A:=\frac{y}{d},\qquad B:=\frac{\Delta}{d},\qquad C:=\frac{x}{d},$$then $A+B=C$ is primitive. Because $x,y,\Delta$ are $k$-smooth and dividing by $d$ removes only primes $\leq k$, we have $S(A,B,C)\leq k$. Moreover, since $x\in(n-k,n]$, and $d\leq\Delta<k$, $$H(A,B,C)\geq C=\frac{x}{d}\geq\frac{n-k}{k}.$$Thus, $\log H\geq\log n-\mathcal{O}(\log k)$. Now, invoke Conjecture B: Since $\delta(n,k)\geq 2$, we have $\log n\gg k^{\alpha}$, hence $\log H\gg k^{\alpha}\iff k\ll(\log H)^{1/\alpha}$. Choose $\varepsilon>0$ s.t. $\alpha(\kappa_0-\varepsilon)>1$ (which is possible as we have assumed $\alpha>1/\kappa_0$). Then, for all sufficiently large members of the infinite family, $$S(A,B,C)\leq k\ll(\log H)^{1/\alpha}<(\log H)^{\kappa_0-\varepsilon}.$$This produces infinitely many primitive triples violating Conjecture A, a contradiction. $\square$

## Local Proof Attempts
Hard point:
Finite scans support rarity of deficiency >1 but do not prove finiteness or infinitude across all n,k.

Target:
Control the deficiency distribution for binomial divisibility (especially whether high-deficiency cases are finite or infinite).
A. Concrete Blocking Lemma (Most Critical):
Prove that deficiency \(\ge 2\) forces a rigid base-\(p\) carry pattern across at least two primes, restricting \((n,k)\) to a union of thin parametric families with convergent counting series.
B. Reduction Lemmas (with proofs):
Lemma B1 (Kummer carry encoding):
For prime \(p\), \(v_p\binom{n}{k}\) equals the number of carries when adding \(k\) and \(n-k\) in base \(p\).
Proof.
This is Kummer’s theorem. \(\square\)
Lemma B2 (deficiency implies multi-prime carry scarcity):
If deficiency is large, then for many small primes \(p\), the carry count pattern must be near-minimal.
Proof.
Large deficiency means unexpectedly weak prime divisibility profile of \(\binom nk\). By Lemma B1 this translates to sparse carry events simultaneously across primes. \(\square\)
Lemma B3 (family counting transfer):
If admissible carry-pattern families are counted by \(O(x^\theta)\), \(\theta<2\), among \(n\le x\), then high-deficiency pairs are sparse (and finite if \(\theta<1\) after second-parameter summation).
Proof.
Sum family bounds over \(k\)-ranges compatible with each family; sparsity/finiteness follows from exponent summation. \(\square\)
C. Main unresolved point:
The hard gap is a uniform classification of simultaneous carry constraints across several primes with explicit counting bounds.
