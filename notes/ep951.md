# EP-951

## Problem Statement
Let $1<a_1<\cdots$ be a sequence of real numbers such that\[\left\lvert \prod_i a_i^{k_i}-\prod_j a_j^{\ell_j}\right\rvert \geq 1\]for every distinct pair of non-negative finitely supported integer tuples $k_i,\ell_j\geq 0$. Is it true that\[\#\{ a_i \leq x\} \leq \pi(x)?\]

## Site Remarks
Erd\H{o}s \cite{Er77c} said this question was asked 'during [his] lecture at Queens College [by] one member of the audience (perhaps S. Shapiro)'. (Although in \cite{Er80} he seems sure it was Shapiro, but also recalled that he had himself asked this in \cite{Er69}.) In \cite{Er80} he further asks whether equality holds if and only if the $a_i$ are the set of primes.

Such a sequence of $a_i$ is sometimes called a set of Beurling prime numbers (and the sequence of products called the associated generalised integers).

Beurling conjectured that if the number of reals in $[1,x]$ of the form $\prod a_i^{k_i}$ is $x+o(\log x)$ then the $a_i$ must be the sequence of primes.

It is unclear whether this is intended to hold for all $x$ or just all sufficiently large $x$. The former question can be disproved by finite calculation (since any finite sequence of $a_i$ can be extended to a suitable infinite sequence via a greedy algorithm). A finite counterexample for $x=10$ was found by ChatGPT-5.2 Pro (prompted by Leeham).

  Nice result. The numerical computations in particular need to be verified, but there does not seem to be any theoretical reason why this would not work, though as Thomas points out the proof technique relies heavily on being able to produce a counterexample in the bounded $x$ regime (in fact $x=10$ in this argument) rather than the asymptotic regime $x \ggg 1$. This changes the game considerably, and the AI proof observes that one can now convert the problem to one in Diophantine approximation.
  
  The first observation is that once one is committed to producing a counterexample at a single value of $x$, the choices of $a_i$ larger than $x$ are pretty much irrelevant; they can be constructed by a standard greedy algorithm. So now one is looking at a multiplicative semigroup generated by a bounded number of generators $\{ a_i \leq x \}$, which is expected to become exponentially sparse at infinity (cf. Størmer's theorem (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St%C3%B8rmer%27s_theorem) or Baker's theorem (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baker%27s_theorem)). But as the AI observes, one does not need the fanciest transcendence theorems such as Baker's theorem involving logs of algebraic numbers, because the $a_i$ are not required to be algebraic; instead, by taking the $a_i$ to be exponentials of algebraic numbers, one now just needs good lower bounds on Diophantine approximation by algebraic numbers, for which the most classical of bounds - Liouville's bound (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diophantine_approximation#Approximation_of_algebraic_numbers,_Liouville) - is already powerful, giving lower bounds that are polynomial in the height (after extending to higher-dimensional approximation in a standard fashion), whereas to get unit separation one needs lower bounds that are at worst exponentially decaying in the height. This is a lot of room, and already guarantees unit separation for all sufficiently large products, reducing things to a finite check. (This already shows by the way that merely assuming asymptotic unit separation would not be enough to get the required bound; if the problem had any hope of a positive answer, one would need unit separation at all scales, not just the large ones.)
  
  A human at this point would perhaps balk at the significant numerical experimentation one would now have to do to find a set of generators for which the required finite number of separation conditions actually hold; but apparently an AI (prompted sufficiently many times) has much less reluctance to attempt some sort of search (possibly brute force or trial-and-error in nature). What is not currently clear is how much "room" there is here - would any reasonable set of generator choices in a reasonable algebraic number field have had a good chance to succeed? Perhaps further numerical exploration will clarify this.

## Local Proof Attempts
New partial result (integer-valued subcase):
If all a_i in [1,x] are integers, then the target bound holds:
#{a_i <= x} <= pi(x).
Reason:
- write each integer a_i as its exponent vector over primes <=x,
 v_i in Z^{pi(x)};
- if there is a nontrivial integer relation sum c_i v_i = 0, splitting positive
 and negative parts gives two distinct exponent tuples with equal products,
 violating the separation hypothesis;
- so {v_i} must be Z-linearly independent, hence its size is at most the
 ambient rank pi(x).
So any counterexample (if one exists) must use genuinely non-integer reals.
Formal theorem (integer case):
Fix \(x\ge2\), and let \(a_1,\dots,a_m\in\{2,\dots,\lfloor x\rfloor\}\) satisfy the separation condition
\[
\left|\prod_i a_i^{u_i}-\prod_i a_i^{v_i}\right|\ge 1
\]
for all distinct exponent vectors \(u,v\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}^m\).
Then
\[
m\le \pi(x).
\]
Proof:
Let \(p_1,\dots,p_{\pi(x)}\) be primes \(\le x\).
Write
\[
a_j=\prod_{r\le \pi(x)} p_r^{e_{r,j}},
\]
and set column vectors \(v_j=(e_{1,j},\dots,e_{\pi(x),j})\in\mathbb Z^{\pi(x)}\).
If \(\{v_j\}_{j=1}^m\) were linearly dependent over \(\mathbb Z\), there exists
\[
\sum_{j=1}^m c_j v_j=0,\qquad c_j\in\mathbb Z,\ \text{not all }0.
\]
Split \(c_j=c_j^+-c_j^-\) with \(c_j^\pm\in\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\), so
\[
\sum_j c_j^+ v_j=\sum_j c_j^- v_j.
\]
Translating back to prime exponents gives
\[
\prod_j a_j^{c_j^+}=\prod_j a_j^{c_j^-},
\]
with distinct exponent vectors \(c^+\neq c^-\), contradiction to separation.
Hence \(v_1,\dots,v_m\) are \(\mathbb Z\)-linearly independent in \(\mathbb Z^{\pi(x)}\), so \(m\le \pi(x)\).

Obstacle:
These are finite truncation checks only. They strongly suggest the separation
condition is extremely restrictive for dense real sets, but do not yet yield a
full theorem for all real sequences.
