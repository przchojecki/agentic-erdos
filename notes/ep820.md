# EP-820

## Problem Statement
Let $H(n)$ be the smallest integer $l$ such that there exist $k<l$ with $(k^n-1,l^n-1)=1$.

Is it true that $H(n)=3$ infinitely often? (That is, $(2^n-1,3^n-1)=1$ infinitely often?)

Estimate $H(n)$. Is it true that there exists some constant $c>0$ such that, for all $\epsilon>0$,\[H(n) > \exp(n^{(c-\epsilon)/\log\log n})\]for infinitely many $n$ and\[H(n) < \exp(n^{(c+\epsilon)/\log\log n})\]for all large enough $n$?

Does a similar upper bound hold for the smallest $k$ such that $(k^n-1,2^n-1)=1$?

## Site Remarks
Erd\H{o}s \cite{Er74b} proved that there exists a constant $c>0$ such that\[H(n) > \exp(n^{c/(\log\log n)^2})\]for infinitely many $n$.

van Doorn in the comments sketches a proof of the lower bound: that there exists some constant $c>0$ and infinitely many $n$ such that\[H(n) > \exp(n^{c/\log\log n}).\]The sequence $H(n)$ for $1\leq n\leq 10$ is\[3,3,3,6,3,18,3,6,3,12.\]The sequence of $n$ for which $(2^n-1,3^n-1)=1$ is A263647 (https://oeis.org/A263647) in the OEIS.

See also [770].

- 12:54 on 15 Oct 2025 - Woett:
  The proof by Erdős combined with slightly more recent knowledge provides the required lower bound on $H(n)$ for infinitely many $n$, modulo the exact value of the constant $c$. Let me explain.
  
  Let $\omega^*(n)$ count the number of primes $p$ such that $p-1$ divides $n$. By Proposition $10$ in this (https://math.dartmouth.edu/~carlp/PDF/paper37.pdf) 1983 paper by Adleman, Pomerance and Rumely (see page $201$, or page $29$ of the pdf-file), there exists an absolute constant $c > 0$ such that there are infinitely many $n$ with $\omega^*(n) > n^{c/\log \log n}$.
  
  So let $n$ be a large enough integer for which $m > n^{c/\log \log n}$ primes $p_1, p_2, \ldots, p_m$ exist with $p_i - 1 | n$. With $k < l = H(n)$ such that $\gcd(k^n-1, l^n-1) = 1$, we then must have that $kl$ is divisible by the product $p_1p_2 \cdots p_m$ due to Fermat's little theorem. By noting that this product $p_1p_2\cdots p_m$ is, by the prime number theorem, larger than $e^{2m}$ if $n$ (and therefore $m$) is large enough, we get
  \begin{align*}
  H(n) &> \sqrt{kl} \\
  &\ge \sqrt{p_1p_2 \cdots p_m} \\
  &> e^m \\
  &> \exp[n^{c/\log \log n}],
  \end{align*}as promised.
  
  By the way, since $\omega^*(n)$ is at most the number of divisors of $n$, which is in turn upper bounded by $n^{(\log 2 + o(1))/\log \log n}$, it is tempting to think that the optimal $c$ could be $\log 2$. But I certainly have no idea and this is pure speculation.

## Local Proof Attempts
Core reduction used:
H(n)=3 is exactly equivalent to
gcd(2^n-1,3^n-1)=1,
since l=3 forces k=2.
Define for each prime p>3:
m_p:= lcm(ord_p(2), ord_p(3)).
Then the equivalent arithmetic form is:
H(n)=3 <=> n is not divisible by any m_p.
Reason: p | gcd(2^n-1,3^n-1) iff both orders divide n, i.e. iff m_p | n.

Conditional theorem (proved as reduction):
If the divisor-moduli family {m_p: p>3} is not a covering system of the positive integers
(equivalently: infinitely many n avoid all m_p), then H(n)=3 infinitely often.
So the infinitude problem is reduced to a covering-system question for {m_p}.
