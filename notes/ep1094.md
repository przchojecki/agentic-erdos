# EP-1094

## Problem Statement
For all $n\geq 2k$ the least prime factor of $\binom{n}{k}$ is $\leq \max(n/k,k)$, with only finitely many exceptions.

## Site Remarks
A stronger form of [384] that appears in a paper of Erd\H{o}s, Lacampagne, and Selfridge \cite{ELS88}. Erd\H{o}s observed that the least prime factor is always $\leq n/k$ provided $n$ is sufficiently large depending on $k$. Selfridge \cite{Se77} further conjectured that this always happens if $n\geq k^2-1$, except $\binom{62}{6}$.

The threshold $g(k)$ below which $\binom{n}{k}$ is guaranteed to be divisible by a prime $\leq k$ is the subject of [1095].

More precisely, in \cite{ELS88} they conjecture that if $n\geq 2k$ then the least prime factor of $\binom{n}{k}$ is $\leq \max(n/k,k)$ with the following $14$ exceptions:\[\binom{7}{3},\binom{13}{4},\binom{23}{5},\binom{14}{4},\binom{44}{8},\binom{46}{10},\binom{47}{10},\]\[\binom{47}{11},\binom{62}{6},\binom{74}{10},\binom{94}{10},\binom{95}{10},\binom{241}{16},\binom{284}{28}.\]They also suggest the stronger conjecture that, with a finite number of exceptions, the least prime factor is $\leq \max(n/k,\sqrt{k})$, or perhaps even $\leq \max(n/k,O(\log k))$. Indeed, in \cite{ELS93} they provide some further computational evidence, and point out it is consistent with what they know that in fact this holds with $\leq \max(n/k,13)$, with only $12$ exceptions.

Discussed in problem B31 and B33 of Guy's collection \cite{Gu04} - there Guy credits Selfridge with the conjecture that if $n> 17.125k$ then $\binom{n}{k}$ has a prime factor $p\leq n/k$.

This is related to [1093], in that the only counterexamples to this conjecture can occur from $\binom{n}{k}$ with deficiency $\geq 1$.

There is an interesting discussion about this problem on MathOverflow (https://mathoverflow.net/questions/368183/updates-on-a-least-prime-factor-conjecture-by-erdos).

## Local Computation
- exhaustive scan .
- For each pair $(n,k)$ with $4\le n\le N_{\max}$ and $2\le k\le n/2$, computed exact least prime factor of $\binom{n}{k}$ via p-adic valuations.
- Compared all detected exceptions against the known 14-term exception set.

Deep run (DEPTH=4) with
\[
N_{\max}=2600
\]
checked all eligible pairs and found:
- exception count = 14,
- no missing known exceptions in range,
- no new exceptions beyond the known 14 in range,
- probe counts remain at 14 all the way to $n=2600$.
This is strong finite evidence for the finite-exception conjecture, but not a proof beyond scanned range.

## Local Proof Attempts
Target:
Prove that
\[
\operatorname{lpf}\!\binom{n}{k}\le \max\!\left(\frac nk,k\right)
\]
for all \(n\ge 2k\), with only finitely many exceptions.
A. Concrete Blocking Lemma (Most Critical):
Show that any exception must satisfy a simultaneous digital-carry obstruction in all primes \(p\le \max(n/k,k)\), forcing \((n,k)\) into finitely many explicit parametric families.
B. Reduction Lemmas (with proofs):
Lemma B1 (least-prime-factor criterion):
\(\operatorname{lpf}\binom{n}{k}>B\) iff \(p\nmid\binom nk\) for every prime \(p\le B\).
Proof.
By definition, least prime factor exceeds \(B\) exactly when no prime \(\le B\) divides the number. \(\square\)
Lemma B2 (Lucas/Kummer divisibility test):
For each prime \(p\), divisibility \(p\mid\binom nk\) is decidable by base-\(p\) digits (Lucas), equivalently by positive carry count (Kummer).
Proof.
Lucas gives \(\binom nk\not\equiv0\pmod p\) iff each base-\(p\) digit of \(k\) is \(\le\) corresponding digit of \(n\). Kummer gives the carry formulation. \(\square\)
Lemma B3 (A implies finite-exception theorem):
If Lemma A reduces exceptions to finitely many families and each family is finite effectively, then only finitely many exceptions exist.
Proof.
Finite union of finite sets is finite. \(\square\)
C. Main unresolved point:
The key challenge is proving that simultaneous nondivisibility by all small primes cannot persist outside boundedly many structured carry configurations.
