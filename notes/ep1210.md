# EP-1210

## Problem Statement
Let $A\subseteq [1,n)$ be a set of integers such that $(a,b)=1$ for all distinct $a,b\in A$. Is it true that\[\sum_{a\in A}\frac{1}{n-a}\leq \sum_{p<n}\frac{1}{p}+O(1)?\]

## Site Remarks
In \cite{Er80} he claims he 'did not state [this] quite correctly' in \cite{Er77c}. The problem in \cite{Er77c} which Erd\H{o}s is presumably referring to states that if $n<q_1<\cdots<q_k\leq m$ is the set of primes in $(n,m]$ then\[\sum \frac{1}{q_i-n}< \sum_{p<m-n}\frac{1}{p}+O(1).\]See also [460] and [950].

- 14:50 on 08 Apr 2026 - Thomas Bloom:
  GPT Pro suggests that this should be deducible fairly quickly from standard results in sieve theory. The key point is to prove that $\lvert A\cap [n-x,n)\rvert\leq \pi(x)+O(x/(\log x)^2)$ for all $x$, from which this follows by partial summation. The contribution from those $a\in A\cap [n-x,n)$ with a prime factor $<x$ is at most $\pi(x)$, since any prime $p\leq x$ can divide at most one such $a$. So it suffices to bound the number of $a$ which are $x$-rough, which it claims is $\ll x/(\log x)^2$.
  
  I haven't checked the details, so it may be being a little optimistic here, but it seems to perform such arguments well, so presumably this works out; I'll let others with more experience confirm this (or, even better, formalise this!)
  
  EDIT: Actually I am a little sceptical now, since $A$ may contain many primes in $[n-x,n)$, and it seems difficult to show there is not a large contribution from such primes. (cf. [855]).

  I also believe this doesn't work.
