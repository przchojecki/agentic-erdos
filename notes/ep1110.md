# EP-1110

## Problem Statement
Let $p>q\geq 2$ be two coprime integers. We call $n$ representable if it is the sum of integers of the form $p^kq^l$, none of which divide each other.

If $\{p,q\}\neq \{2,3\}$ then what can be said about the density of non-representable numbers? Are there infinitely many coprime non-representable numbers?

## Site Remarks
A problem of Erd\H{o}s and Lewin \cite{ErLe96}, who proved that there are finitely many non-representable numbers if and only if $\{p,q\}=\{2,3\}$.

Indeed, in \cite{Er92b} Erd\H{o}s wrote 'last year I made the following silly conjecture': every integer $n$ can be written as the sum of distinct integers of the form $2^k3^l$, none of which divide any other. He wrote 'I mistakenly thought that this was a nice and difficult conjecture but Jansen and several others found a simple proof by induction.'

This simple proof is as follows: one proves the stronger fact that such a representation always exists, and moreover if $n$ is even then all the summands can be taken to be even: if $n=2m$ we are done applying the inductive hypothesis to $m$. Otherwise if $n$ is odd then let $3^k$ be the largest power of $3$ which is $\leq n$ and apply the inductive hypothesis to $n-3^k$ (which is even).

Yu and Chen \cite{YuCh22} prove that the set of representable numbers has density zero whenever $q>3$ or $q=3$ and $p>6$ or $q=2$ and $p>10$. They also prove that there are infinitely many coprime non-representable numbers if $q>3$ or $q=3$ and $p\neq 5$ or $q=2$ and $p\not\in \{3,5,9\}$.

Erd\H{o}s and Lewin \cite{ErLe96} also asked whether all large integers $n$ can be written as a sum of $2^k3^l$, none of which divide another, each of which is $>f(n)$ for some $f(n)\to \infty$. Let $f(n)$ be the fastest growing such $f(n)$. Yu and Chen \cite{YuCh22} proved\[\frac{n}{(\log n)^{\log_23}}\ll f(n) \ll \frac{n}{\log n}.\]Yang and Zhao \cite{YaZh25} improved the lower bound to $f(n)\gg n/\log n$. van Doorn has observed in the comments that a result of Blecksmith, McCallum, and Selfridge \cite{BMS98} already implies\[f(n)\sim \frac{\log 2\log 3}{2}\frac{n}{\log n}.\]The case of three powers is the subject of [123], and see also [845] for more on the case $\{p,q\}=\{2,3\}$. The problem [246] addresses the topic without the non-divisibility condition.

- Terms are represented by exponent pairs $(a,b)$; non-divisibility is antichain condition in product order.
- Enumerated representable sums up to cutoff $N$ exactly per tested pair (all runs here finished, no timeouts).

Deep run (DEPTH=4) used:
- $N=50{,}000$,
- 105 ordered coprime prime pairs $(p,q)$ with $p>q$ from

\]
Highlights:
- Control pair $(3,2)$ had non_count = 0 in this finite window.

\[

Finite evidence strongly aligns with the known dichotomy between the exceptional $

## Local Proof Attempts
Target:
For coprime \(p>q\ge2\), classify density and coprime-subset behavior of non-representables under antichain sums of \(p^a q^b\), separating exceptional \(\{2,3\}\) from generic pairs.
A. Concrete Blocking Lemma (Most Critical):
Prove an antichain-sum sparsity theorem: for \(\{p,q\}\neq\{2,3\}\), representable numbers up to \(N\) are at most \(N^{1-\delta_{p,q}}\) (or explicit zero-density upper bound with power saving).
This would immediately imply density 1 for non-representables and sharpen quantitative claims.
B. Reduction Lemmas (with proofs):
Lemma B1 (poset reformulation):
Allowed summand sets correspond to antichains in \(\mathbb N^2\) under coordinatewise order via \((a,b)\mapsto p^a q^b\).
Proof.
\(p^{a_1}q^{b_1}\mid p^{a_2}q^{b_2}\) iff \(a_1\le a_2\) and \(b_1\le b_2\). Non-divisibility among chosen terms is exactly antichain condition. \(\square\)
Lemma B2 (counting via rank layers):
Number of candidate antichain terms with value \(\le N\) is \(O((\log N)^2)\), and any antichain has size \(O(\log N)\).
Proof.
Bounds on \(a,b\) are \(a\le \log_p N\), \(b\le \log_q N\). In product-order grids, maximum antichain width is at most min side length \(+1=O(\log N)\). \(\square\)
Lemma B3 (A implies density dichotomy):
If representables are \(o(N)\), then non-representables have density 1; combining with existing exceptional-case theorem yields full qualitative dichotomy.
Proof.
Non-representable count is \(N-\#\text{repr}(N)\). If \(\#\text{repr}(N)=o(N)\), quotient tends to 1. \(\square\)
C. Main unresolved point:
The central challenge is turning combinatorial antichain counting into sharp additive-sum counting with cancellation of collisions.
