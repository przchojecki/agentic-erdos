# EP-1071

## Problem Statement
Is there a finite set of unit line segments (rotated and translated copies of $(0,1)$) in the unit square, no two of which intersect, which are maximal with respect to this property?

Is there a region $R$ with a maximal set of disjoint unit line segments that is countably infinite?

## Site Remarks
A question of Erd\H{o}s and T\'{o}th. The answer to the first question is yes (which Erd\H{o}s gave Danzer \$10 for). There is no prize mentioned in \cite{Er87b} for the second question.

There are two examples Erd\H{o}s gives in \cite{Er87b}, the {IMAGE=1071-one,first} by Danzer, the {IMAGE=1071-two,second} by an unnamed participant.

In \cite{Er87b} he further asks what happens if the unit line segments are rotated/translated copies of $[0,1]$ that are allowed to intersect only at their endpoints.

Alexeev has proved in the comments that the unit square itself admits a countably infinite maximal such set.

  It seems this proves this works for any region $R$ that is a parallelogram with sides $1$ and $a < 1$. The proof is that if $ABCD$ is such a parallelogram with $AB=CD=1$, $BC=DA<1$, $\angle DAB \le \pi/2$, then construct $E$ on $CD$ with $AE=1$ and $F$ on $AB$ with $CF=1$. We have $AFCE$ is also a parallelogram with this property, and the construction continues. Moreover, all constructed parallelograms share a diagonal with length between $1$ and $2$; we also put one line segment on this diagonal.

  Oh, sorry! I forgot I set my posts to be viewable only when logged in. Here’s a copy of the thread:
  
  I think one way to make this well posed is to assume that all of our lines of length 1 are *open* intervals of length one, so there are no endpoints to worry about.
  
  With this formulation, there is a way of getting a maximal collection that is countable, using only line segments of one of three types: those having one endpoint at (0, 0), those having one endpoint at (1/2, 1), and those having one endpoint at (1, 0).
  
  The first step is to draw a vertical line from (1/2,0) to (1/2,1), and to then take care of each half-square separately.
  
  Now suppose we have a finite collection of non-intersecting open unit lines in the left half that divide the left half into a bunch of scalene triangular regions and one parallelogram, where the longest side of each triangle has length 1, and the parallelogram has (0,0) and (1/2,1) as vertices, and its longest sides also have length 1.
  
  A scalene triangle whose longest side has length 1 cannot contain a unit line of length 1 other than that longest side, so we only have to figure out what to do with the parallelogram.
  
  The base case: The parallelogram is the whole left side.
  
  See the figure. [Here is a copy of the figure: https://ewhowe.com/images/ErdosFigure1.jpg ]
  
  Now draw a line of length 1 from (0,0) to the length-1 side of the parallelogram that touches (1/2,1). And likewise, draw a line of length 1 from (1/2,1) to the length-1 side of the parallelogram that touches (0,0). These are shown in red.
  
  We’ve divided the black parallelogram into two scalene triangles with maximum side length 1, plus a new parallelogram — the one with two red sides — that still has (0,0) and (1/2,1) as vertices and that has maximum side length 1.
  
  Now repeat. This figure shows the new parallelogram from the preceding figure, now in black. Repeating the construction gives two new red sides of length 1. Again we get 2 scalene triangles with max side length 1 and a new parallelogram with (0,0) and (1/2,1) as vertices and with max side length 1.
  [The second figure: https://ewhowe.com/images/ErdosFigure2.jpg ]
  
  It seems clear that if we repeat this we’ll get a countable sequence of nested parallelograms that rapidly converge to the diagonal connecting (0,0) to (1/2,1). So taking all the length-1 lines we drew, plus a length-1 segment of the diagonal, we’ll get a maximal set for the left half of the square.
  
  Do the same for the right hand side, and we’re done!

## Local Computation
- deep geometric-combinatorial experiment for the finite side:
 - random candidate generation of unit segments inside $[0,1]^2$,
 - exact conflict-graph MIS via bounded branch-and-bound,
 - large number of independent trials,
 - sampled extension stress test to probe near-maximality.
- Deep run scale:
 - $4000$ trials,
 - $348$ candidates per trial,
 - runtime about $113$ seconds.

- Best finite packing found in this candidate model was size $26$ among $348$ candidates.
- Across probes, many trial winners were still extendable by additional random segments, indicating this random candidate pool is not globally maximal in geometric space.
- This is consistent with the finite question being positive but does not address the open countably-infinite maximal-family problem.

## Local Proof Attempts
Closed Subquestion:
The finite maximal-family existence question is solved positively in the cited
background.
Remaining Open Subquestion:
Existence of a region supporting a countably infinite maximal disjoint unit
segment family remains open.
- Need a geometric compactness/extension principle that either forces
 extendability of every infinite disjoint family, or constructs a rigid
 infinite maximal one.

Target:
Resolve whether some region admits a countably infinite maximal family of
pairwise disjoint unit segments.
Step A: Compactness Dichotomy
Prove an infinite disjoint family in bounded region has either:
1. an accumulation geometry enabling extension, or
2. a rigid limit-configuration class.
Step B: Extension Criterion
Show every family in class (1) is not maximal (construct one extra disjoint
unit segment).
Step C: Rigidity Resolution
Either construct explicit maximal example from class (2), or prove class (2)
cannot occur.
Concrete Blocking Lemma (Most Critical):
Need a local extension lemma: if the complement region around accumulation
directions has positive angular-width corridor, then one can insert an
additional disjoint unit segment.
